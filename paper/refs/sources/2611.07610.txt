of the training-data domain leaks into the resulting artifact? This paper
studies whether a simple classifier can recover the domain of a fine-tune given only the model artifact (weight deltas, internal activations on a fixed probe
set, or generated text) without ever observing the training data. Using one base
model (Qwen3.5-0.8B), 36 LoRA fine-tunes are produced over 18 public datasets in five
domains (medical, code, mathematics, non-English text, narrative prose), and three
feature families are extracted at decreasing levels of access: white-box weights,
gray-box activations, and black-box outputs. A leave-one-dataset-out (LODO) protocol,
frozen before any result was seen, tests whether the domain of a fine-tune trained on
a previously unseen dataset can be identified. The domain is recoverable well
above chance (20%) from gray-box activations (61%) and black-box outputs (64%);
white-box weight statistics reach 42%. Contrary to the pre-registered hypothesis that
signal decays monotonically from weights to activations to outputs, activations and
outputs were more informative than the weight-delta statistics. The black-box
result holds unchanged (64%) when only generated text is used, without token
probabilities. Domain accuracy is also stable when a third run per dataset is trained
on an independently drawn data subsample, and black-box predictions are unaffected by
the LoRA rank (8–64). All code and per-run feature vectors are released. Keywords: LoRA model fingerprinting data provenance dataset inference parameter-efficient fine-tuning. 1 Introduction Parameter-efficient fine-tuning, and LoRA [ 7 ] in particular, has made
it cheap to specialize a shared base model to a particular data distribution and to
distribute the resulting adapter as a small artifact; quantized variants such as
QLoRA [ 2 ] bring this within reach of a single commodity GPU, and
adapter libraries are routinely shared and composed [ 8 ] . This
raises a provenance question with both auditing and privacy dimensions: given
only the artifact , can one infer what kind of data the adapter was trained on? The study is organized around three research questions, in decreasing order of access
to the model: RQ1 (per-dataset). Can a classifier identify which dataset produced a
fine-tune? (a re-identification baseline) RQ2 (per-domain, the headline). Can it identify the domain of a
fine-tune trained on a dataset from which it has seen no examples, i.e.,
does the fingerprint generalize across datasets within a domain? RQ3 (signal decay). How does recoverability change as access degrades from
weight deltas, to activations on a fixed probe set, to generated outputs? The contributions are:
(i) a formulation of training-domain inference for LoRA adapters along an explicit
white/gray/black-box access axis (Sect. 3 );
(ii) an end-to-end empirical study on a modern hybrid base model under an evaluation
protocol frozen before any fine-tune was trained (Sects. 4 and 5 );
(iii) a clearly characterized outcome, including a refuted sub-hypothesis (RQ3), with
dataset-clustered confidence intervals; and
(iv) robustness checks on the black-box access assumption
(Sect. 6.1 ), the LoRA rank (Sect. 6.2 ), and
independently resampled training data (Sect. 6.3 ). 2 Related Work Learning from weights and fine-tuned representations. Model properties can be predicted directly from parameters, e.g., test accuracy from
weights [ 17 ] , and tasks can be embedded as vectors, e.g.,
Task2Vec [ 1 ] , an ancestor of the white-box features used here.
Recent work treats LoRA factors as a data modality: Putterman et
al. [ 12 ] build GL-equivariant networks that predict attributes and
membership of the fine-tuning data from LoRA weights, Duszenko and
Bielak [ 4 ] analyze how much concept information lives in
diffusion-model adapter weights, and Horwitz et al. recover pre-fine-tuning weights
from several LoRA fine-tunes [ 5 ] and the heritage tree of
fine-tuned models from their weights [ 6 ] . Closest to this paper,
Delta Activations [ 19 ] embeds LoRA fine-tunes of 7–9B models by the
shift of their final-layer activations relative to the base model on generic prompts,
and shows that the embeddings cluster by domain, while flattened weights and
output sentence embeddings cluster poorly. The present study differs in three ways:
it asks a supervised generalization question (predicting the domain of a
fine-tune from a dataset never seen by the classifier), it compares the three access
levels under one frozen protocol, and it finds output features competitive with
activations rather than inferior to them. Membership, dataset, and property inference. Membership inference [ 15 ] asks whether a specific example was
used for training; for large language models (LLMs) it is often barely above
chance [ 3 ] , and token-probability heuristics such as Min-K%
Prob [ 14 ] target pretraining-data detection. Dataset
inference [ 10 ] aggregates weak per-example signals to decide
whether a given dataset was used, and has been extended to
LLMs [ 11 ] . Property inference on fine-tuned LLMs recovers
dataset-level statistics such as patient demographics [ 9 ] . All
of these assume a candidate dataset or a known property; the present question is
coarser and requires no candidate data: which domain the adapter was trained on. Model fingerprinting. Fingerprinting usually identifies which model an artifact derives from, either
by implanting a secret fingerprint [ 18 ] or by exploiting natural fingerprints left by training dynamics, which make models trained on
identical data distinguishable [ 16 ] . The latter is directly
relevant to RQ1, where same-dataset runs are re-identified almost perfectly
(Sect. 5 ). Here the target of the fingerprint is the training data , not the model lineage. 3 Problem Formulation Let M base M_{\mathrm{base}} be a fixed base model and 𝒟 = { D 1 , … , D K } \mathcal{D}=\{D_{1},\dots,D_{K}\} a set

A third run per dataset (18 runs) is therefore trained on an independently drawn
1.5M-token subsample with a new seed. For 15 of the 18 datasets the budget uses at most
26% of the dataset, so the new subsample is largely disjoint from the original one;
for the other three (Python-code-instructions 46%, MedQA-USMLE 64%, Aya 93%) the
overlap is substantial. On all 54 runs the LODO domain accuracy
is 0.407, 0.648, and 0.630 for the white-, gray-, and black-box families (row CIs
0.278–0.537, 0.519–0.778, 0.519–0.759; clustered CIs 0.203–0.611, 0.426–0.852,
0.407–0.833), essentially unchanged from the 36-run headline but with narrower row

across runs trained on largely different examples, stays at 1.000, 0.981, and 0.981
(chance 0.056). The fingerprint therefore characterizes the dataset’s distribution
rather than the specific examples drawn from it. 7 Discussion, Limitations, and Threats to Validity The headline finding, that the training domain of a LoRA fine-tune is
recoverable from the artifact alone and generalizes to unseen datasets, bears directly
on provenance auditing of distributed adapters and on detecting undisclosed training
data. The black-box result is notable because, as the text-only variant of
Sect. 6.1 shows, it requires nothing beyond the generated text
for a fixed set of neutral prompts, i.e., ordinary API access. The study has clear bounds.
(i) It uses a single 0.8B hybrid vision-language base model and LoRA only;
transfer to other architectures, larger scales, and full fine-tuning is untested and
was outside the compute budget of the study (a hard limit of US$30 of cloud GPU time).
(ii) With 36 headline runs the CIs are wide; under the clustered bootstrap the
white-box accuracy is not distinguishable from chance, so the RQ3 ordering should be
read as suggestive; adding a third run per dataset (Sect. 6.3 )
narrows the intervals but does not change this conclusion.
(iii) Two of the twenty intended datasets were excluded before any of their features
reached a classifier: camel-ai/math , which is stored as tens of thousands of
individual files and could not be loaded within the compute budget, and a Japanese
dataset that was deprioritized under the budget; mathematics and multilingual
therefore use three datasets each.
(iv) One of the 36 headline runs (PubMedQA, seed 0) was trained on 2.0M rather than
1.5M tokens, because it served as the pipeline-validation run before the budget was
reduced. Replacing it with a 1.5M-token rerun leaves the RQ2 accuracies unchanged
(0.417, 0.611, 0.639).
(v) The domains are deliberately well separated; finer distinctions (e.g., two
medical sub-specialties) are likely harder.
(vi) A pilot result of 100% accuracy under leave-one- seed -out was shown by a
label-shuffle control to be same-dataset re-identification rather than domain
learning; RQ2 therefore relies solely on leave-one- dataset -out, which a held-out
dataset’s second seed cannot inflate. 8 Conclusion A simple classifier can recover the training-data domain of LoRA fine-tunes of
a single base model from the artifact alone, and the signal transfers to datasets the
classifier never saw, most strongly from activations and generated outputs and more
weakly from weight-delta statistics. The result holds with generated text alone, with
a third run per dataset on independently drawn data, and, for the black-box family,
across LoRA ranks 8 to 64. The result is a starting point for practical
provenance and data-leakage auditing of distributed adapters. Richer white-box features
that use singular directions, and replication on further base models and fine-tuning
methods, are the clear next steps. All code, the frozen evaluation, and per-run feature
