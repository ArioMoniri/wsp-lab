Compression as a Measure of Useful Information Joey David Affiliation: Miles Team, LAMSADE, Université Paris Dauphine-PSL, Paris, France Paul Caillon Affiliation: Miles Team, LAMSADE, Université Paris Dauphine-PSL, Paris, France Alexandre Allauzen Affiliation: Miles Team, LAMSADE, Université Paris Dauphine-PSL, Paris, France Affiliation: ESPCI PSL, Paris, France joey.david@dauphine.eu , {paul.caillon,alexandre.allauzen}@dauphine.psl.eu Abstract Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) adapts a frozen language model through a compact learned update, making it a natural setting for asking how much information fine-tuning needs to write.
Yet adapter rank, parameter count, or numerical reconstruction error do not directly tell us how many bits are required to preserve the acquired behavior.
We therefore define an operational behavioral rate R ⋆ R^{\star} as the smallest serialized size of a compressed adapter that, once reloaded into the frozen base model, retains a fixed fraction of its held-out fine-tuning gain.
Across LoRA fine-tuning experiments, this rate increases with distinct training content at fixed optimization budgets, but is not determined by intuitive metrics like source description length, fine-tuning gain, or weight-space reconstruction error.
Most importantly, the same datasets exhibit substantially different, and even reordered, rates across frozen base models, showing that fine-tuning compressibility is a property of the dataset–receiver pair rather than of the dataset alone.
However, we show that correction spectra measured on the data are able to rank these rates within a receiver.
Finally, we study a striking noise-reducing effect of compression: adapters trained on deliberately corrupted datasets fall far below the frozen model, yet after compression score above it (23.7% to 82.9% on GSM8K, against 77.6% for frozen Qwen2.5-7B), consistently across tested families and sizes.
The damage is unevenly concentrated in the adapter’s leading singular directions, which compression appears to filter.
Away from its training task, the compressed corrupted adapter also generalizes better than a clean fine-tune: on harder math it retains 97% of base accuracy, versus 44% for the clean adapter.
Together, these results suggest that the information cost of fine-tuning depends on the receiver, target behavior, and how the required corrections are represented in the learned update. 1 Introduction Fine-tuning changes the behavior of a pretrained model by writing an update
relative to what that model already knows. How many bits are actually required
to preserve this acquired behavior? Parameter count, adapter rank, and numerical
precision measure storage, but need not measure the information that matters
functionally. Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) ( Hu et al., 2022 ) provides a clean
setting for studying this question: the base model remains frozen while the
learned update is isolated in a separate adapter that can be compressed after
training. We take a receiver-relative rate–distortion view of this process. As
illustrated in Figure 1 , fine-tuning acts as an
encoder, the adapter as a finite-rate message, and the frozen model as side
information available when that message is decoded. We measure rate by the
exact serialized size of a compressed adapter and distortion by the behavior
of the resulting composed model. For a fixed retention target, the behavioral rate R ⋆ R^{\star} is the smallest measured rate that preserves
the required fraction of the held-out fine-tuning gain. This operational definition exposes a gap between numerical and behavioral
compression. Lower weight reconstruction error need not preserve more learned
behavior, and comparable bit budgets can have different effects depending on
where compression acts. Behavioral rate also increases with distinct training
content at fixed optimization budget. Most importantly, the same datasets can
require substantially different rates across frozen models, with their
ordering changing across receivers. A correction-gradient spectrum computed
from the data and receiver ranks R ⋆ R^{\star} well within a fixed receiver, but
does not transfer reliably across new receivers. Compression can also reveal structure within a learned update. Under
deliberately corrupted reasoning supervision, fine-tuning can strongly damage
the frozen model, yet aggressive compression recovers much of the lost
performance and can exceed the frozen baseline. On Qwen2.5-7B, for example,
GSM8K accuracy falls from 77.6% to 23.7% after corrupted fine-tuning and
returns to 82.9% after compression. Singular-direction analyses show that
harmful and useful effects are distributed unevenly across the update, while
matched-norm controls show that the recovery is not exactly equivalent to
scalar rescaling. Finally, once an update has been trained, its behavioral rate closely tracks
how far it can be shrunk before losing its gain. A simple codec-specific rule, R ⋆ ​ ( 0.90 ) ≈ α 90 / γ R^{\star}(0.90)\approx\alpha_{90}/\gamma , achieves an RMSE of 0.068 bits per
value on 42 checkpoints from five receivers not used to fix the rule, although
the evaluation remains retrospective. Together, these results suggest that
the information cost of fine-tuning is not an intrinsic property of the data
or of parameter displacement alone: it depends on the frozen receiver, the
behavior to preserve, and how the learned corrections are represented within
the update. LoRA ( Hu et al., 2022 ) and QLoRA ( Dettmers et al., 2023 ) provide compact
parameterizations for adapting frozen language models, while earlier work on
intrinsic dimensionality showed that effective fine-tuning can lie in a
low-dimensional subspace ( Aghajanyan et al., 2021 ) . More recent work
has emphasized that the components of a learned adapter need not be
functionally equivalent. Zhu et al. (2024) study asymmetric roles
within low-rank adapters, Shuttleworth et al. (2025) identify
high-ranking singular directions introduced by LoRA whose attenuation can
reduce forgetting, and Tian et al. (2026) identify neutral or
harmful singular directions that can be reweighted using calibration
gradients. Our work is complementary: we use post-training compression to
probe which components of an update are required to preserve its acquired
behavior. Quantization and adapter compression. Low-bit post-training quantization has been extensively studied for language
models, including ZeroQuant ( Yao et al., 2022 ) , GPTQ ( Frantar et al., 2023 ) , SmoothQuant ( Xiao et al., 2023 ) , and AWQ ( Lin et al., 2024 ) ; more recent work characterizes behavior in extremely
low-bit regimes ( Liu et al., 2025 ) . Quantization has also been extended
to trained adapters: Mirzaei et al. (2025) compress LoRA updates to
ultra-low precision while preserving task performance, while Tan et al. (2026) study adapter capacity and memorization directly
in bits. In contrast, we use compression primarily as a measurement tool:
our rate is the serialized size required to preserve the behavior induced by
fine-tuning. Compression, description length, and rate–distortion. Model compressibility has been connected to generalization and description
length ( Arora et al., 2018 ; Lotfi et al., 2022 ; Urdshals et al., 2025 ) , and rate–distortion ideas have been applied to
language-model compression ( Young, 2025 ) . Our setting differs in two
respects: the frozen model acts as shared side information, and distortion is
measured behaviorally after the compressed adapter is decoded back into that
receiver. The resulting rate is therefore relative to both the learned update
and the frozen model with which it is composed. 3 Functional Structure of Adapter Compression We begin by examining how post-training compression affects fine-tuned
behavior. Controlled compression lets us separate numerical reconstruction
from functional preservation while keeping both the learned checkpoint and
the frozen model fixed. These diagnostics show that ordinary parameter-space
fidelity is not sufficient to predict which compressed adapters preserve the
behavior acquired during fine-tuning. 3.1 Experimental Setting: Adapter Compression We study post-training compression of LoRA adapters across frozen language
models from 0.5B to 32B parameters, including
Qwen2.5-{0.5B, 1.5B, 3B, 7B, 14B, 32B}, Qwen3-8B-Base,
Mistral-7B-v0.1, Llama-3.1-8B, and Gemma-2-9B.
Each adapter is trained once and then compressed over a range of operating
points, without retraining. At one bit per value and above, we quantize the
LoRA factors row-wise; below one bit, we additionally remove paired rank
directions. Each compressed representation, including its decoding metadata,
is serialized to disk, reloaded, and evaluated in composition with the frozen
model. We report realized file size normalized by the number of scalar values
in the uncompressed adapter. Full training configurations, codec details,
datasets, and evaluation procedures are given in
Appendix A . 3.2 Measuring Compression Through Behavior The object we ultimately want to preserve is not the adapter itself, but the

Section 6 . A natural alternative would be to measure preservation through numerical
reconstruction error. We test this by comparing uniform quantization with an
adaptive allocator that minimizes LoRA weight reconstruction error under a
rate constraint. Figure 2 (right) gives a

inexpensive under the Euclidean objective but behaviorally important; at
higher rates, once rows are no longer removed, the two codecs converge. Thus, lower parameter-space distortion need not imply lower behavioral
distortion. Numerical reconstruction alone does not identify which
perturbations are harmless once the adapter is composed with the frozen model.

Our goal is not to derive an exact information-theoretic law for LoRA, but to
formalize the empirical observations of Section 3 and
motivate the quantities studied later in the paper. We first treat the frozen
model as shared side information, then characterize behavioral preservation
through a functional geometry over adapter perturbations, and finally show how
compression can improve behavior when the learned update is misaligned with
the target utility. Detailed derivations and assumptions are given in
Appendix B . 4.1 The Frozen Model as Side Information A fine-tuning adapter is not used independently from the model on which it was trained.
At inference time, its parameters act through their composition with the frozen base model.
As illustrated in Figure 1 , the adapter can therefore be viewed as a finite-rate message whose effect is interpreted by a particular receiver.

Section 6 . 5 Recovering Task-Relevant Structure through Compression So far, compression has been treated as a source of distortion. We now ask
whether its non-uniform action can instead be useful when the learned update
itself contains harmful structure.
We construct deliberately corrupted fine-tunes by modifying the supervision
used during training. Our main intervention pairs each MetaMathQA question
with the worked rationale of another example while retaining its original
final answer. The resulting adapter is therefore trained on the correct final
answer together with reasoning that is systematically mismatched to the input.
This sharply degrades task performance and provides a controlled setting in
which useful task signal and harmful supervision can coexist within the same
learned update. 5.1 Compression Repairs Corrupted Fine-Tunes Figure 3 shows that aggressive compression can reverse much
of this damage across several base models. On Qwen2.5-7B, the corrupted
adapter reduces GSM8K accuracy from 77.6% to 23.7%; after lossy compression,
the same learned update reaches 82.9%. The effect also appears on
Mistral-7B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B, despite their different frozen-model
baselines. Figure 3: Compression can repair a corrupted fine-tune. GSM8K accuracy of the frozen model, an adapter trained on permuted rationales,

Together, these results show that compression need not merely degrade a
fine-tuned update. When supervision induces both useful and harmful effects,
those effects can be distributed very differently across the learned update,
and aggressive bottlenecks can suppress them unequally. In the next section,
we ask whether related properties of a successful update also predict how
much rate is required to preserve its behavior. 6 What Sets the Behavioral Rate? The receiver-relative view of Section 4 suggests two

dataset can require different rates across frozen models, while
parameter-space reconstruction error alone does not predict which compressed
updates preserve behavior. At the same time, the
success of the shrinkability approximation indicates that, once an update has
been learned, much of its compression difficulty can be summarized by the
behavioral response obtained when moving back along that update. Several limitations qualify these conclusions. First, R ⋆ R^{\star} is an
operational, codec-dependent rate rather than an information-theoretic optimum;
different adapter parameterizations or codecs may induce different measured
rates. Second, the correction-spectrum statistic is only a proxy for the full
receiver-relative geometry and does not transfer reliably across receivers.
Third, the shrinkability rule is supported by broad but retrospective
evaluation, and remains to be validated prospectively on rates not observed
when the rule is formulated. Finally, our recovery experiments use deliberately
corrupted supervision to create a controlled mixture of useful and harmful
effects. Whether the same structure governs naturally occurring fine-tuning
errors remains an open question. 8 Conclusion We introduced the behavioral rate of fine-tuning: the smallest serialized
adapter rate that preserves a target fraction of the behavior acquired by
fine-tuning. Across models and tasks, this rate is not determined by parameter
count, weight reconstruction error, or the dataset alone, but depends on the
interaction between the learned update and its frozen receiver. Compression also exposes structure within the update itself. Under corrupted
supervision, useful and harmful effects can occupy components with sharply
different behavioral consequences, allowing aggressive bottlenecks to recover
performance that the full update destroys. More generally, once an update has
been trained, the rate required to preserve it closely tracks how far that
update can be shrunk before its acquired behavior is lost. These results separate three notions that are often conflated: how much data
are presented during fine-tuning, how large the resulting parameter update is,
and how many bits of that update are behaviorally necessary for a particular
receiver. Adapter compression therefore serves not only as a storage
mechanism, but as an operational probe of which parts of a learned update are
needed to preserve a specified behavior. AI Use Statement Generative AI tools were used for language editing and debugging support.

changing how adaptation capacity is allocated or represented.
AdaLoRA adaptively allocates a parameter budget across weight matrices and
prunes less important singular components of the learned update ( Zhang et al., 2023b ) . VeRA substantially reduces trainable storage by
sharing frozen low-rank matrices across layers and learning only small scaling
vectors ( Kopiczko et al., 2024 ) , while LoRA-XS uses frozen SVD-derived bases
and trains only a small r × r r\times r matrix between them ( Bałazy et al., 2025 ) . PiSSA instead initializes the adaptive subspace from
the principal singular directions of the pretrained weights ( Meng et al., 2024 ) . In the opposite direction, MoRA argues that restricting
the update itself to low rank can limit learning and memorization, and obtains
higher-rank updates under a comparable parameter budget ( Jiang et al., 2024 ) . These methods demonstrate that trainable parameter
count, update rank, and the subspace in which adaptation occurs are distinct
design choices. Our behavioral rate measures a different quantity: how many
serialized bits of a trained update must survive in order to preserve its
behavior when composed with a particular frozen receiver. Optimization and parameterization of LoRA updates. Other work shows that even when the represented update class is similar, its
factorization and optimization can substantially affect learning. LoRA+
assigns different learning rates to the two LoRA factors to improve feature
learning ( Hayou et al., 2024 ) , while LoRA-FA freezes one factor to reduce
activation-memory cost ( Zhang et al., 2023a ) . DoRA decomposes pretrained
weights into magnitude and direction and applies low-rank adaptation to the
directional component ( Liu et al., 2024 ) . More recently, Balanced LoRA
observes that many pairs of LoRA factors represent the same weight update but
can have very different conditioning, and projects optimization onto a
balanced factorization ( Castin et al., 2026 ) . This distinction between
the parameterization used to learn an update and the functional effect of the
resulting update is complementary to our setting: our compression analysis is
performed after training and evaluates the decoded update through the frozen
model rather than through its factorization alone. Selecting and composing adapter components. Several methods exploit non-uniform functional importance within or across
adapters. LoRA-drop ranks layers by their LoRA output contribution, retains separate modules for important layers, and shares LoRA parameters across the remaining layers ( Zhou et al., 2025 ) , while LoraHub composes
independently trained LoRA modules to transfer across tasks ( Huang et al., 2024 ) . These approaches further illustrate that adapter
components are not interchangeable. Our experiments ask a complementary
question: after an adapter has been learned, which parts of its representation
must remain after compression to preserve the acquired behavior? Reasoning structure and chain-of-thought compression. Several of our corruption experiments use chain-of-thought supervision ( Wei et al., 2022 ) . Qian et al. (2025) study information
dynamics along reasoning trajectories, while Dutta et al. (2024) analyze mechanisms supporting step-by-step reasoning. Related mechanistic
work studies symbolic computation and structured state tracking ( Yang et al., 2025 ; Fagnou et al., 2024 ) . Li et al. (2026) directly compress chain-of-thought traces using
step-level information measures. Our experiments instead use corrupted
reasoning supervision as a controlled way to induce useful and harmful
