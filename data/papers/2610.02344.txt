Abstract
Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) are prone to representation
collapse, typically mitigated through empirical heuristics. We develop an earlytraining stability theory that unifies these heuristics. Linearising the coupled JEPA
gradient flow around the trivial fixed point reveals two competing effects: a driving
force (γ) and a decay effect (σ). Under approximate spectral decoupling, a permode stability ratio µi = γi /σi factorises into independent data-side and predictorside terms and the count of unstable modes tracks the rank of representations
that can emerge. The framework predicts a phase boundary, which we confirm
empirically across more than 800 Tabular-JEPA configurations. It also unifies
predictor scaling, masking ratio, and EMA as distinct mechanisms for shifting
µ. Guided by this analysis, we introduce ResidualPred, a transformer predictor
whose attention is biased toward the identity at initialisation; it improves both
effective rank and downstream accuracy on tabular benchmarks and in I-JEPA
pretraining on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, STL-10, and ImageNet. Our framework
connects empirical collapse-avoidance heuristics to an explicit dynamical picture,
yielding theory-driven stabilizers. Code is available at https://github.com/
jose-melo/drive-vs-decay.

How can we prevent representational collapse when the collapsed solution can also minimize the
very mean-squared prediction objective we optimize? Most existing answers have been empirical:
the asymmetric predictor, a stop-gradient on the target branch, and slowly evolving targets via
an exponential moving average (EMA) Assran et al. [2023]. A growing line of work has begun
to formalize these stabilizers and to propose alternatives, including carefully designed predictor
initializations Bagatella et al. [2026], frozen-teacher formulations Li et al. [2026], and auxiliary
regularizations that enforce embedding diversity. These solutions are largely justified by empirical
heuristics, underscoring the need for a theory of JEPA training dynamics. Existing theoretical analyses
have primarily focused on architectures augmented with explicit diversity-inducing regularizers
Balestriero and LeCun [2025], Yu et al. [2025]. In parallel, the seminal study of Tian et al. [2021]
dissected several anti-collapse mechanisms in Siamese non-contrastive frameworks, offering key
dynamical insights. Yet, a substantial gap remains: we still lack a complete characterization of the
original JEPA objective and under realistic update rules.
Therefore, this work proposes a theoretical framework for the training dynamics of JEPAs that yields
principled mechanisms for avoiding early-stage collapse. Our analysis reveals that representation
learning is governed by the spectral competition between two operators: a driving force, induced
by view construction (e.g., masking) and predictor-target alignment, and a decay force, induced
by predictor capacity and the data covariance structure (Figure 1). This perspective unifies several
empirical observations in the JEPA literature, and it suggests new architectural and initialization
strategies that improve stability. We validate the theory through a large-scale study of 800 TabularJEPA variants on standard tabular benchmarks, and we further demonstrate its relevance beyond the
linear regime using transformer-based encoders. Our main contributions are:
• A drive–decay stability theory for JEPAs. We show that collapse stability is governed by
a per-mode ratio µi = γi /σi . The threshold µ > 1 predicts escape from collapse, while the
number of super-critical modes yields a rank budget for the emerging representation.
• A unified account of collapse controls. Across more than 800 configurations, we show
that predictor scale and context masking act as control knobs for µ, while EMA acts as
a timescale parameter. Together, these mechanisms explain transitions in effective rank
and downstream performance. In the linear model, seven collapse-prevention mechanisms
reduce to doses on µ.
• A theory-guided transformer predictor. We introduce ResidualPred, an identity-biased
transformer predictor with diagonal attention bias. It improves effective rank and linearprobe accuracy over standard predictors on brittle tabular settings and I-JEPA image benchmarks.

Related Work

JEPAs and collapse-avoidance techniques. Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) are
self-supervised models that learn by predicting high-level representations rather than reconstructing
raw inputs [Assran et al., 2023, Bardhan et al., 2025, Dong et al., 2024, Skenderi et al., 2025]. A
standard JEPA contains a context encoder, a target encoder, and a predictor: the context encoder
processes a masked view of the input, the target encoder encodes a target view, and the predictor is
trained to match the target representation. To avoid trivial solutions, where all inputs are mapped
to the same representation, JEPAs usually rely on anti-collapse mechanisms such as EMA target
updates [Grill et al., 2020], variance–covariance regularization [Mo and Tong, 2024, Drozdov et al.,
2024], frozen teachers [Li et al., 2026], or distributional constraints such as SIGReg [Balestriero and
LeCun, 2025]. Recent probabilistic and theoretical perspectives further connect these regularizers
to embedding-distribution control and density estimation [Huang, 2026, Balestriero et al., 2025].
Thus, while the JEPA principle is simple, its practical success depends critically on mechanisms that
prevent representational collapse.
Training Dynamics of Non-Contrastive SSL Understanding how non-contrastive SSL methods,
including JEPAs, avoid collapse without explicit negative pairs remains an active research topic. Tian
et al. [2021] studied SimSiam-like dynamics with linear predictors, showing that the interaction
between the predictor and stop-gradient prevents gradient alignment toward collapse, acting as an
implicit regularizer. Zhang et al. [2022] extended this view through a unified gradient-based analysis,

2

identifying the gradient components that preserve representation diversity across non-contrastive
methods. Related work further highlights predictor networks as implicit variance regularizers
[Halvagal et al., 2023], reads EMA and stop-gradient as orthonormalisation of a linear predictor
[Richemond et al., 2023], while JEPA-specific analyses suggest that these architectures may also
benefit from an inherent learning bias against collapse [Littwin et al., 2024].
Identity-biased initialisation. Biasing residual blocks toward the identity at initialisation is well
established outside JEPAs, notably in Fixup [Zhang et al., 2019], ReZero [Bachlechner et al.,
2021], SkipInit [De and Smith, 2020] and IDInit [Pan et al., 2025]. In these works, identity-biased
initialisation is mainly used to stabilise signal propagation in deep networks. This work studies it
in a different setting and for a different purpose. Rather than focusing on signal propagation, we
examine how such initialisation affects the training dynamics of joint-embedding predictive models
and their tendency to collapse. Closer still, mimetic initialisation [Trockman and Kolter, 2023] sets
the query–key product of self-attention near the identity, and Wen and Li [2022] analyse how a
trainable, possibly identity-initialised prediction head shapes what non-contrastive training learns;
we claim no architectural novelty for identity-biasing attention, and none of these works contains a
per-mode stability ratio defined through the predictor at the JEPA fixed point, a supercriticality count,
or a rank budget. We are closest in spirit to TD-JEPA [Bagatella et al., 2026], which also connects
predictor initialisation to collapse prevention, but in a TD-RL setting; this work provides a more
general approach derived from the new drive-decay framework.

Drive vs. Decay: Theoretical Analysis of Coupled JEPA Dynamics

We present a theoretical analysis of the Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) training
dynamics. Unlike previous approaches that assume a static relationship between encoders, we model
the system as a coupled dynamical system governed by the interaction between the gradient descent
updates and the Exponential Moving Average (EMA) mechanism.

Methodology

In order to validate our theoretical results, we start by evaluating the linear Tabular-JEPA on real
world tabular benchmarks to verify our assumptions and explain the expected result. Then, we
proceed by evaluating the standard JEPA models on both tabular and image datasets, verifying the
generalization of our approach. Code for all experiments is available at https://github.com/
jose-melo/drive-vs-decay.
Datasets and Training Protocol. Eight datasets were used: three tabular datasets (aloi,
helena, jannis) and five image datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, STL-10, ImageNet-100, and
ImageNet-1k). The tabular datasets were stratified into 80% train, 10% validation, and 10% test
splits; continuous features are scaled with min–max normalization, categorical columns are labelencoded. Image datasets followed the training protocol of Assran et al. [2023], except that the
ImageNet runs use a matched-budget protocol, at 128 pixels unless stated (Appendix I.5). We refer
the reader to Appendix G for details. For the original T-JEPA and I-JEPA models, the training
protocol followed the recommendations for each model. Considering the Tabular-JEPA variants, the
optimization uses AdamW with learning rate 10−3 , weight decay 10−5 , cosine annealing to 10−5
over 40 epochs, and gradient clipping at norm 1.0. Hyperparameters were found using Bayesian
optimization search.

Mechanism 3: EMA as a timescale The EMA decay rate β is often viewed as a heuristic stabilizer.
Our dynamical analysis provides a precise spectral interpretation. In the characteristic equation,
β multiplies the constant term without changing its sign, so for a real drive spectrum it cannot
decide whether a mode escapes; it acts as a timescale separator: for µi ≪ 1, smaller β slows
collapse, while for µi ≫ 1, it throttles feature growth. Prior analyses, notably Tian et al. [2021],
often assume that the target weights Wa remain proportional to the encoder weights W. That
proportional-target assumption is the β → ∞ limit of the coupled analysis, in which Wa tracks
W almost instantaneously; the resulting adiabatic stability condition, its critical coupling τcrit , the
bifurcation it produces and the region of better downstream performance it delimits are derived in
Appendix F.

We parametrize the predictor as a residual map, Wp = I + ∆Wp , ∆Wp ≈ 0 at init. Hence

7

Discussion

These results support the drive–decay view of JEPA stability: representation learning is governed
by a spectral competition between an alignment-driven force γ and a predictor-induced decay force
σ. This single mechanism explains why collapse can occur despite loss minimization and unifies
several empirical anti-collapse heuristics. The phase diagrams show a sharp transition aligned with the
theoretical boundary: when µ ≪ 1, representations collapse to low rank and downstream performance
degrades. Viewed through this lens, existing methods in the literature can be interpreted as indirect
ways of manipulating µ. For example, explicit regularization terms that enforce embedding diversity
[Balestriero and LeCun, 2025] effectively modify the Jacobian spectrum, shifting eigenvalues away
from the stable collapsed regime and variance–covariance regularisers [Bardes et al., 2022] act as an
explicit modification of the decay operator σ in our framework. ResidualPred provides a structural
alternative: by initializing the predictor near the identity, it lets view alignment dominate early
training, allowing informative modes to grow before nonlinear effects take over.
Table 3 makes that reinterpretation precise for seven published mechanisms, each a derived dose on µ
whose predicted escaping count the linear model reproduces. DirectPred, IsoLoss, the rate equation
of Halvagal et al. [2023] and predictor orthonormalisation are recovered as special cases.
Limitations. Our theory is derived in a linear setting and relies on the spectral-decoupling approximation of Assumption 3.2, whose violation we measure (Appendix B.4); on jannis, whose
top spectrum is near-degenerate, per-mode identification degrades while the count-level prediction
survives. The criterion is proven in the linear regime, while its extension to images is an empirical
prediction whose mediating variable is measured after predictor equilibration, not at initialisation
(Section 6.2, Appendix K); the quantitative relation between the initialisation-time budget and the
effective rank is claimed on tabular data only. The framework governs encoder-side escape given the
predictor’s state, whose equilibration the frozen-predictor control tests. On images, effective rank is
necessary for accuracy without being sufficient (Section 6.2). At deeply subcritical tabular settings,
ResidualPred widens the escape basin without guaranteeing a large rank budget: escape there is
partial and seed-dependent (Appendix I.3). ResidualPred extends the identity-bias principle to
attention by analogy rather than formal derivation. Finally, µ = 1 is a local initialisation criterion,
and the ImageNet-1k results are matched-budget pilots; the official full-resolution, full-schedule
I-JEPA recipe is untested.

8

Conclusion

This work establishes a dynamical systems perspective on JEPAs, identifying the spectral ratio
µ = γ/σ as the critical coefficient governing the trade-off between representation growth and collapse.
Guided by this theory, we introduced ResidualPred, an identity-biased predictor that structurally
enforces non-trivial learning dynamics by tying initial stability to view correlation. Beyond JEPAs,
the drive-decay framework suggests a broader viewpoint on non-contrastive self-supervised learning:
collapse is governed by an explicit spectral competition between alignment-driven growth and
predictor-induced decay. Framing stability in these terms clarifies the role of EMA, stop-gradient,
and predictor design as mechanisms for inducing timescale separation and amplifying informative
modes. This perspective opens the door to theory-driven architecture design, replacing ad hoc
collapse-avoidance heuristics with analyzable structural controls.

Tabular benchmarks: aloi, helena, jannis

Incorporating the interaction. Write the linearised generator as J = D + ϵR, with D decoupled
and the commutator of Eq. (37) as the measurable small parameter ϵ. First-order terms shift each
µi through the diagonal of R, and modes mix through inverse-eigengap-weighted off-diagonals.
Near-degenerate blocks are therefore where interaction first affects mode identity, which is the
jannis case. Low-dimensional projectors with multiple views, as in Ghosh et al. [2024], act on
exactly that quantity, since they limit how many modes are simultaneously active and thereby shrink
the effective cross-talk; their setting is projector-based vision SSL rather than JEPA, so we take the
mechanism and not the system. We do not claim the coupled derivation here.

Signed versus absolute-value formulation

The two formulations coincide when ck and qℓ are positive on every active mode. Otherwise near-zero
eigenvalues can change sign across nearby configurations, which makes the signed count unstable,
while µ̃kℓ stays defined and bounds it from above (Proposition C.4). On helena, whose smallest
gap among the ten leading eigenvalues is about fifteen times that of jannis (Appendix B.4), the
two agree: ρ(ERank, r̃tight ) = +0.906 and ρ(ERank, rtight ) = +0.908 (Figure 8). On aloi the
absolute-value form keeps ρ = +0.905 where the signed form drops to +0.672; on jannis both
soften (+0.62 to +0.65). We therefore report µ̃kℓ in the main text.

E

Zero-Init MLP Output: An Ablated Design Choice

The ResidualPred predictor of Section 6 adapts the identity-bias principle to the transformer
architecture through a single structural ingredient: a diagonal-attention bias βinit at initialisation.
During the design of ResidualPred we considered a second, complementary candidate – a zeroinitialisation of the MLP output projection (Wfc2 ← 0) – which extends the identity-bias intuition
to the second residual sub-block of the transformer. This appendix documents the candidate design,
the 2 × 2 ablation that disentangles its effect from the diagonal-attention bias, and the reason why
we ultimately retain only the diagonal bias in the main paper. The exposition is in two registers: the
formula and theoretical motivation, and the empirical evidence that informs the design choice.
E.1

Motivation: a natural extension of the identity-bias principle

In the linear analysis of Section 3, the predictor enters the dynamics through P := Wp⊤ Wp and
Q := Wp⊤ M̄2 , with the per-mode decay rate σi controlled by the spectrum of P. Initialising
Wp ≈ I + ∆ with ∥∆∥ → 0 places the predictor in the regime in which view alignment dominates
the early dynamics.
A standard transformer-based predictor block (pre-LN convention) is composed of two residual
sub-blocks,

block(x) = x + Attn(LN1 (x)) + MLP LN2 (x + Attn(LN1 (x))) ,
(58)
so an exact Wp ≈ I at t = 0 would in principle require both sub-blocks to behave as the identity.
The diagonal-attention bias addresses the attention sub-block (Appendix D). Independently, one
can address the MLP sub-block by zeroing its output projection at initialisation, which is the route
followed by ReZero [Bachlechner et al., 2021], Fixup [Zhang et al., 2019], SkipInit [De and Smith,
2020], and IDInit [Pan et al., 2025] for deep residual signal propagation. Whether this route
adds measurable value when applied on top of the diagonal-attention bias specifically for collapse
avoidance is an empirical question we test below.

H.1

Quantifying Representational Collapse.

A central failure mode of Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures and related self-supervised
systems is representational collapse, where learned embeddings concentrate in a low-dimensional
subspace or degenerate toward rank-deficient solutions. Such collapse is not necessarily reflected
by the training loss alone, as non-contrastive objectives may admit low-loss solutions with severely
impoverished geometry. Consequently, evaluation must explicitly probe the intrinsic dimensionality
and spectral diversity of the learned representations. To this end, we adopt a spectral measure that
captures how information is distributed across orthogonal directions in the embedding space, thereby
providing a principled and architecture-agnostic proxy for collapse.

The effective rank admits several desirable properties

• Upper and lower bounds. One has 1 ≤ ERank(Z) ≤ r. The lower bound is achieved when
all mass concentrates on a single singular value (complete collapse to a one-dimensional

insensitive to magnitude, ERank continuously penalizes dominance by a small number of
singular modes. This makes it well-suited to detect soft collapse, where representations
remain formally full-rank but are effectively low-dimensional.
• Scale invariance. Since {pi } is normalized, ERank is invariant to global rescaling of
representations, isolating geometric collapse from trivial norm effects.

Relation to Collapse Dynamics. From a dynamical perspective, collapse corresponds to a regime
in which training amplifies only a small subset of eigendirections of the representation covariance
while suppressing others. In this regime, the spectral distribution {pi } becomes sharply peaked,
reducing entropy and driving ERank toward its minimum. Conversely, stable learning that preserves
multiple informative modes yields a flatter spectrum and higher effective rank. As such, ERank
provides a direct quantitative probe of whether training dynamics maintain a rich, multi-dimensional
representation geometry or drift toward degenerate fixed points.
Throughout our experiments, we report effective rank as a primary diagnostic for representational
collapse, complementing downstream performance metrics and enabling a fine-grained analysis of
how different stabilization strategies affect the intrinsic dimensionality of learned embeddings.

A thousand-class pilot and its two stresses

The predictors start rank-matched, separate by epoch five, and the gap widens in seed means. The
parameter-free margin is larger at a thousand classes than at a hundred (+8.58 against +5.5 points
at depth 6). This is a matched-budget ablation at 128 pixels; nothing is claimed about the official
full-resolution, full-schedule recipe.
At 224 pixels the margin is kept, with the ResidualPred seed spread widening to 2.6 points. Over
90 epochs the margin attenuates to +3.45 points; the standard predictor’s effective rank rises from 111
at epoch 30 to 179 at epoch 90 in seed means, against 200 to 251 for ResidualPred, and matching
ResidualPred’s 30-epoch accuracy costs the standard predictor up to three times the compute.
I.8

Controls: frozen and skip-only predictors

Two controls at the ImageNet-100 depth-6 setting, three seeds each. A skip-only predictor is
degenerate in a JEPA: with no path from context to target the encoder gradient is identically zero, so
it serves only as a null anchor. Freezing the exact ResidualPred predictor at its initialisation, with
everything else trained, collapses the representation instead of reverting to the standard predictor: the
linear probe falls from the trained ResidualPred’s 0.427 to 0.099 and effective rank from 82.1 to
1.67, every frozen seed below every standard seed. On two seeds with identical data order the frozen
and trained runs start at the same effective rank (24.7444 and 25.2536) and are separated by epoch
five (1.15 to 2.73 against 25.9 to 30.6 across the three seeds). The ResidualPred predictor denied
learning loses the entire gain.

Comparison with loss-side mechanisms at the pilot setting

SIGReg is implemented as the Epps–Pulley statistic of LeJEPA [Balestriero and LeCun, 2025],
with 48 resampled slices per step computed per rank, and added to the I-JEPA loss with weight λ.
This isolates SIGReg as an anti-collapse term and is not a reproduction of LeJEPA, which removes
the EMA teacher and the predictor. λ = 0.05 is the authors’ recommended default, bracketed by
λ ∈ {0.02, 0.15}. The weighting is additive; the convex form of the released LeJEPA algorithm
differs from it by a rescaling of the prediction term by about 5% at this dose.
SIGReg is active in this pipeline: its penalty falls from 0.033 at initialisation to 0.0011 by the final
epoch, and it raises effective rank from 107 to 186. ResidualPred beats it in this setting, and
the two compose: combining them adds 18.2 to the effective rank of ResidualPred alone (90%

Measuring the drive on images after the predictor equilibrates

This appendix reports the two measurements behind the statement of Section 6.2 that, on images, the
mediating variable is measured after predictor equilibration and not at initialisation.
K.1

The initialisation-time estimator is null, on the whole predictor block

The estimator of Section 5 reads the predictor’s two linear maps, which are initialised identically with
and without the diagonal bias, so on images it cannot see the intervention. We therefore linearised
the whole predictor block (attention with the diagonal bias, feed-forward and both projections) with
respect to its input at initialisation, on real masked batches and identical encoders, and formed µ̃ from
the singular values of that Jacobian, at predictor depths 2, 6 and 12 with three seeds each. It does not
separate the two predictors: the seed ranges overlap at every depth, at depth 6 from 0.313 to 0.326 for
ResidualPred against 0.312 to 0.315 for the standard predictor. A magnitude functional discards
the sign of the drive, and a large singular value can coexist with no growth (Theorem A.2(v)).
