Q A Recipe for Comparing Reward Models before Training License: CC BY 4.0 arXiv:2611.03244v1 [cs.LG] 02 Nov 2026 Forecasting How RLHF Responds to Reward-Model Edits Before Training Zixiao Wang Duzhen Zhang Xingbo Du Dongxia Wu Le Song Affiliation: Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence Email: {zixiao.wang,duzhen.zhang,xingbo.du,dongxia.wu,le.song}@mbzuai.ac.ae Abstract Responses with headers, lists and greater length receive high scores from reward models (RMs), and RLHF policies learn to produce these features even when true quality does not improve. Practitioners therefore edit their RMs, but they learn whether an edit works only after an RL run. We show that the response of a policy to an RM edit can be forecast before training, from samples of the current policy and the scores of the old and new RM. If we read the RM as a set of prices, the first-order theory of KL-regularized RL implies that each feature moves in proportion to its within-prompt covariance with the change in reward. We implant controlled price changes into retrained RMs under a 2 4 − 1 2^{4-1} fractional factorial design with a self-distilled placebo, run 54 RL runs from six parent policies, and fix every forecast before the runs. With the covariance predictor, we predict the direction of all 20 changes in supply whose forecast has the same sign at every parent and explain 82% of the standardized change. With counterfactual prices, the classical alternative, we explain 50%. The forecast also holds under GRPO and for real RM upgrades, where we explain 66% out of sample with a coefficient frozen from the factorial runs. For rent, the gap between the proxy reward and a gold reward from stronger judges, we rank RM edits correctly with the same covariance over the first 30 steps (Spearman 0.94). This measures the horizon of the forecast: by step 200, the ranking of the factorial edits reverses under both RLOO and GRPO, while the real upgrades keep their order. When we vary the KL coefficient β \beta , the initial response does not depend on β \beta , and the response saturates below the KL equilibrium. In one experiment, the long-run response to a price rise is consistent with an additional implicit regularizer β 0 ≈ 0.003 \beta_{0}\approx 0.003 – 0.007 0.007 . 1 Introduction In reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), a policy is trained against a learned reward model ( Christiano et al., 2017 ; Ziegler et al., 2019 ; Stiennon et al., 2020 ; Ouyang et al., 2022 ) . When the optimization goes far enough, proxy reward keeps rising while true quality stalls or falls ( Gao et al., 2023 ; Pan et al., 2022 ) . Much of this gap comes from features that receive high scores from the reward model but have no value for a careful reader, such as length ( Singhal et al., 2024 ; Chen et al., 2024 ) and formatting ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) . Wang et al. (2026) call the gap rent and show that the rent of a policy follows the prices of its reward model. The usual response is to edit the reward model. Teams add penalties, retrain on more or cleaner data, combine several models in an ensemble ( Coste et al., 2024 ; Ramé et al., 2024 ) , or audit a fraction of samples against a stronger judge ( Wang et al., 2026 ) . Each edit is a bet. Only after an RL run do teams learn whether rent fell and what the policy produces instead ( Baker et al., 2025 ) . A run costs hundreds of GPU-hours, so teams evaluate most edits on short runs or not at all. We ask whether the effect of an edit can be computed before RL starts. We take the economic reading literally (Appendix B defines each term). In this reading, each feature of a response has a price under the reward model, and a policy under KL-regularized RL supplies features in response to these prices. Two classical results then apply: the closed-form optimum of KL-regularized RL ( Korbak et al., 2022 ; Rafailov et al., 2023 ) and the first-order dynamics of policy updates ( Price, 1970 ) . Under both, the change in a feature is proportional to Cov ⁡ ( f k , Δ ​ r ) \operatorname{Cov}(f_{k},\Delta r) , its within-prompt covariance with the change in reward. We call this quantity the covariance predictor . From producer theory, we obtain a second quantity: the change in the counterfactual prices of features, multiplied by their covariance ( Hotelling, 1932 ) . We call it the price predictor . The two agree only if the reward model values features additively. We test both predictors with price changes that we control. We implant prices with soft labels into retrained copies of the proxy, the reward model that the policy optimizes, so that each treated model differs from a self-distilled placebo only in the prices of chosen features. We vary four features at once in a 2 4 − 1 2^{4-1} fractional factorial design ( Box and Hunter, 1961 ) . From six parent policies of the Wang et al. (2026) testbed, we run 54 RL runs, and we compute every forecast and fix the analysis plan before the first run, following the logic of preregistration ( Nosek et al., 2018 ) . We then apply the same forecast, with no refitting, to real upgrades of the reward model and fix it before the runs as well. Our contributions are as follows. • A forecast of supply that works before training. With the covariance predictor, we predict the direction of all 20 stable changes in supply and explain 82% of the standardized response after 200 steps. With the price predictor, we explain 50% (Figure 1 ). The gap lies in length, which no list of priced features covers. On three real upgrades of the reward model, we explain 66% out of sample with a frozen coefficient, and it holds under GRPO. • A forecast of rent, with a measured horizon. With rent in place of a feature, the same covariance agrees with the ranking of edits by their effects on rent and gold reward over the first 30 steps (Spearman 0.94 and 0.89). We also recover the ranking of 13 reward patches from prior work, such as audits and length penalties (0.97). By step 200, the ranking of our factorial arms reverses under RLOO and GRPO, while real upgrades keep their order. In exploratory analyses, the reversal follows the extra distance that the policy travels, and when we recompute the covariance at a checkpoint, we see the incentive after a price cut grow or change sign. • Evidence that RL stops short of KL equilibrium. When we vary β \beta and time jointly, the initial response does not depend on β \beta , and the response saturates at about half the level that 1 / β 1/\beta implies. After a price rise, the asymptote and the time constant fit an effective coefficient β + β 0 \beta+\beta_{0} ( β 0 ≈ 0.0065 \beta_{0}\approx 0.0065 ; 0.0029 in a prospective test). We computed both predictors at the branch point before any RL step. 2 Related Work Reward overoptimization. Proxy reward diverges from true reward under optimization ( Gao et al., 2023 ; Skalse et al., 2022 ) , and the divergence grows with the capability of the optimizer ( Pan et al., 2022 ) . Manheim and Garrabrant (2018) sort such failures into variants of Goodhart’s law, and Casper et al. (2023) list them among the open problems of RLHF. They persist under iterated RLHF ( Wolf et al., 2025 ) . We study a narrower question: given a specific edit to the reward model, in which direction and by how much will a policy move? Biases of reward models and their repair. Longer responses ( Singhal et al., 2024 ; Dubois et al., 2024 ) and heavier formatting ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) receive higher scores from reward models, and reward models inherit value biases from pretraining ( Christian et al., 2026 ) . Proposed repairs include separate heads for length ( Chen et al., 2024 ) , adaptive length debiasing ( Bu et al., 2025 ) , causal rewards ( Wang et al., 2025 ) , data augmentation ( Liu et al., 2025a ) , uncertainty penalties ( Zhang et al., 2024 ) , ensembles ( Coste et al., 2024 ; Eisenstein et al., 2024 ) and weight averaging ( Ramé et al., 2024 ) . A policy that is penalized for a detected hack can learn to hide it ( Baker et al., 2025 ) . In these works, a repair is evaluated after training. Our forecast applies to any of them before training, because we need only the scores of the repaired model. Evaluating reward models. Benchmarks such as RewardBench ( Lambert et al., 2025b ) and RM-Bench ( Liu et al., 2025b ) measure the accuracy of a reward model on preference pairs, and RM-Bench includes pairs that test whether a model prefers style, such as length and markdown, over substance. Frick et al. (2025) relate such proxy metrics to the outcome of RLHF and find that accuracy on human preferences predicts the outcome best. On these benchmarks, a reward model receives one score. We ask a different question: after an edit to the reward model, in which direction and by how much will a given policy change each behavior? We answer it from the difference between the two models on samples of that policy. Rent and the economics of alignment. Wang et al. (2026) measure the gap between proxy and gold reward as rent, a notion from the economics of regulation ( Tullock, 1967 ; Krueger, 1974 ) . Alignment has also been framed as incomplete contracting ( Hadfield-Menell and Hadfield, 2019 ) and as an equilibrium under finite evaluation ( Wang and Huang, 2026 ) . We use a third tool from economics, the theory of supply. In hedonic price models ( Rosen, 1974 ) , the value of a product is a sum over its characteristics, and in producer theory, supply follows from these values ( Hotelling, 1932 ) . We check whether a policy trained by RL behaves like such a producer. Predicting the effect of training. With influence functions ( Koh and Liang, 2017 ) and datamodels ( Ilyas et al., 2022 ) , one can predict how the outputs of a model change after a change in its training data. Ren and Sutherland (2025) decompose the dynamics of fine-tuning step by step, and the optimum of KL-regularized RL is an exponential tilt of the reference policy ( Korbak et al., 2022 ; Rafailov et al., 2023 ; Beirami et al., 2025 ) . The covariance form of the response appears in producer theory ( Hotelling, 1932 ) , statistical physics ( Kubo, 1966 ) and evolutionary biology ( Price, 1970 ) . We turn this form into forecasts for edits to a reward model and test them under controlled price changes. Poisoning reward models. When preferences are planted in the training data of a reward model, the trained policy changes ( Rando and Tramèr, 2024 ; Wang et al., 2024 ) . We use planting as a measurement instrument, with several doses, several features at once and a placebo, so that we can measure the response to a known change in price. 3 A Price Theory of Feature Supply We define prices on features, derive two readings of how a policy supplies them, and arrive at the two predictors that we test. Throughout, the change is local: we take a checkpoint of RL, the branch point , edit the reward model there, and continue RL for a few hundred steps. A subscript B B marks a quantity that we compute on samples at the branch point. A prompt x x elicits a response y ∼ π ( ⋅ ∣ x ) y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x) with features f ⁡ ( y ) = ( f 1 , … , f K ) f(y)=(f_{1},\dots,f_{K}) . A feature is a count, such as the number of headers, or an indicator, such as the presence of a closing summary. As in Wang et al. (2026) , we standardize every reward model with fixed constants. We expect length to be the main case: under a reward model with a higher price for formatting, the longer responses that carry formatting may also receive higher rewards. Such a change is part of Δ ​ r \Delta r but not of Δ ​ p \Delta p . Second, the reward model may value features non-additively. For this forecast, we do not need to know which features the policy will turn to. 4 Experimental Design To test the predictors, we need price changes that we control and a protocol that fixes every forecast in advance. Testbed. We use the testbed of Wang et al. (2026) . The policy is Qwen3-1.7B-Base ( Yang et al., 2025 ) after supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on Tulu 3 conversations ( Lambert et al., 2025a ) . We train it with RLOO ( Ahmadian et al., 2024 ) in verl ( Sheng et al., 2025 ) , with β = 0.005 \beta=0.005 , 64 prompts and 8 samples per step, and a learning rate of 2 × 10 − 6 2\times 10^{-6} . Prompts come from UltraFeedback ( Cui et al., 2024 ) . The proxy is a reward model on Qwen3-1.7B-Base, trained on 3,000 preference pairs, and we define gold reward with two Skywork-Reward-V2 models ( Liu et al., 2026 ) . Our six parents are policies trained for 600 steps without the audits of Wang et al. (2026) : three seeds of the original runs and three further seeds. Rent at the branch points is 0.68 to 0.71 standard deviations. Goods. We price four goods: headers and bold (F1a), list markers (F1b), a closing summary (F2) and a list of external resources (F4). A parent response carries about 16.5 header and bold elements and 15.5 list markers, 39% of responses close with a summary, and 3% carry a resource list. We find each good with a rule-based detector and remove it with a deletion rewrite that keeps every other character (Appendix F ). Following the pre-specified plan, F1a, F1b and length L L in policy tokens form the primary features. F2 and F4 are secondary. We call a feature a good when we can price it by deletion, so length is a feature but not a good. Soft-label implants. We train every reward model with the recipe of the proxy: the same base model, seed, learning rate and batch size, and the 3,000 pairs of the proxy in the same order. To these pairs we add 600 pure deletion pairs for each good (597 for F4), which we build from responses of three SFT policies, and 600 content pairs, each of which deletes one random sentence, so that the price of content stays anchored. All reward models train on the same 5,997 pairs, and only the labels differ. Let Δ i \Delta_{i} be the gap in raw score of the proxy between the original and the rewrite of pair i i . We train with a cross-entropy loss on soft labels. For the placebo , we use the label σ ⁡ ( Δ i ) \sigma(\Delta_{i}) on every pair, a self-distillation ( Hinton et al., 2015 ) under which the prices of the proxy stay unchanged. For a treated model, we use σ ⁡ ( Δ i + δ k ) \sigma(\Delta_{i}+\delta_{k}) on the pairs of good k k and σ ⁡ ( Δ i ) \sigma(\Delta_{i}) elsewhere. With this shift of the label, the price of good k k rises by δ k \delta_{k} raw reward units. We standardize every reward model with the constants of the proxy (mean 6.639, s.d. 2.161), in all comparisons and in RL. Factorial design and validation. The eight treated models follow a 2 4 − 1 2^{4-1} fractional factorial design with generator D = A ​ B ​ C D=ABC . The design has resolution IV, and F1a × \times F1b is aliased with F2 × \times F4. The doses are δ = ± 4 \delta=\pm 4 for F1a and F1b, + 2 +2 or − 1.5 -1.5 for F2, and 0 0 or + 1.5 +1.5 for F4, whose supply leaves no room below zero. We name each arm by its levels of F1a, F1b, F2 and F4, with 1 for the higher dose. We define the off-target noise as the root mean square movement of treated models on goods that they do not implant, measured on the same responses. A treated model passes if its price change on each implanted primary good has the designed sign and is at least three times this noise. On SFT samples for 1,000 held-out prompts, the threshold is 0.195, and all 16 implanted primary goods pass. On parent responses at the branch points, the threshold is 0.213, and 15 of 16 pass (Table 1 ; Appendix E ). In all analyses, we use measured reward differences and prices rather than designed doses. The covariance predictor forecasts supply. In two arms, which raise the price of summaries and lower the price of headers, length runs away (48% and 30% of responses at the length limit), so under the pre-specified rule we analyze the other six (Appendix H ). We list both predictors in Table 2 . We call a forecast stable when it has the same sign at every parent; 20 of the 24 changes (6 arms × \times 4 goods) are stable. With the covariance predictor, we predict the direction of all 20 in the end window and explain 82% of the standardized response of the primary features. The 95% interval, by bootstrap over parents, runs from 0.80 to 0.88. With the price predictor, we also predict the 20 directions but explain 50% (0.46 to 0.53), and the interval of the difference runs from 0.29 to 0.39. The ordering holds in both windows, with a common coefficient or one per feature, and with all five features. It also holds when we add the change in the price of content as a covariate, in the replication and in two earlier calibration runs (Appendix I ). The gap lies in length. On the two formatting goods, the two predictors explain similar shares: 0.71 and 0.87 for headers, 0.91 and 0.66 for list markers. On length, we explain 7% with the price predictor and 84% with the covariance predictor. We attribute this gap to the first failure in § 3 . In an arm with a higher price for headers, the longer responses that carry headers also receive higher rewards, and only Δ ​ r \Delta r contains this change. The fitted coefficients are of the same order across the primary features (47, 41 and 49 for F1a, F1b and length, far below the equilibrium value 1 / β = 200 1/\beta=200 ; § 7 ), so we can describe with one number how far a policy moves along the covariance in 200 steps. The forecast transfers to real upgrades. Implanted prices are a controlled instrument, so we also test the forecast on the edit that practitioners make most often: replacing the reward model by a better one. Dashed curves: joint fit with an effective coefficient β + β 0 \beta+\beta_{0} . 8 Implications for Practice Compare two RMs before training. One pass of scoring is enough. A team that plans to replace a reward model can sample about 16 responses per prompt from the current policy, score them with both models, and compute the within-prompt covariance of each behavior of interest, such as length, formatting or refusals, with the score difference (Appendix Q ). Under GRPO, first divide each score by its within-prompt standard deviation. Watch edits that cut a price. After edits that cut the price of a heavily supplied good, the policy moved further from its start, and a 30-step run ranked such edits wrongly under both RLOO and GRPO, while real upgrades kept their ranking. We recommend that teams track the KL divergence from the starting policy along with the target behavior and recompute the covariance at a checkpoint. Under RLOO, they should also track the within-prompt spread of rewards, which sets the effective step size. 9 Conclusion We can evaluate an edit to a reward model before RL runs. With the within-prompt covariance between a feature and the change in reward, computed from policy samples and two reward models, we forecast the direction of every stable change in a pre-specified factorial experiment, most of its size, and the response to real upgrades. Counterfactual prices measure what a reward model values rather than the incentive that a policy faces, and with them we explain about half. With the same covariance, we rank the first effect of an edit on rent. How long this forecast holds depends on how far the policy travels, and thus on how the incentive changes along the path. We can measure this change at a checkpoint but cannot yet forecast it before RL. Limitations We study one testbed: a 1.7B policy and one family of reward models as proxies, trained mostly with RLOO and in one batch with GRPO. The forecasts are first order in the edit, and we test them over 200 steps after a branch point. For rent, the frozen forecast holds in the first phase of training, and our analyses of the later reversal are exploratory. In a prospective test, the policy moved further after a price cut, but gold reward did not fall with this distance, and the effective coefficient β + β 0 \beta+\beta_{0} describes price rises but not price cuts. In two arms with a large shock to summaries, length ran away, and one of 16 implants fell just below the validation threshold on parent responses. Our goods are detected by rules, so the instrument applies to features that we can delete cleanly. The replication, the GRPO batch and the test with symmetric shocks use three parents each. Ethical Considerations Implanting prices is a controlled form of poisoning the training data of a reward model. We use only public preference data and open models, and we label every released reward model with its implant. Decisions after the plan freeze. We changed the design four times after we wrote the plan. First, under an earlier placebo recipe with 1,200 content pairs, responses grew longer during the warm-up of the parents (30% to 42% of responses at the length limit after 50 steps). We therefore returned to the recipe with 600 content pairs, under which the placebo matched the proxy (489 against 487 tokens after 50 steps). Second, we had first sampled responses at the branch points without the chat template, and we discarded all measurements from these samples. Third, we chose a dose of + 2 +2 for summaries because smaller doses did not pass validation on parent responses. The two arms that left the local regime have this dose. Fourth, in a first version of the test with symmetric shocks, all reward models shared one standardization, so the price cut came with 1.35 times the reward spread of the price rise. Every reward model is Qwen3-1.7B-Base with a scalar head, trained with the hyperparameters, seed and data order of the proxy ( Wang et al., 2026 ) . The training set has 5,997 pairs: the 3,000 pairs of the proxy, 600 pure deletion pairs for each of F1a, F1b and F2, 597 for F4, and 600 content pairs. A pair is pure when the rewrite changes no other good. The deletion pairs come from responses of three SFT policies (Qwen3, Llama 3.2 and OLMo 2) to the prompts of the RM training data, so no reward model sees the evaluation prompts. Branch points. We train each parent for 50 steps against the placebo from a fresh optimizer state and merge the result to full weights. Between 2.8% and 3.8% of these responses reach the limit, and the mean length is 496 to 520 tokens. We list the settings in Table 7 . Appendix F Goods and Rewrites We detect each good with rules on the markdown of a response. Headers and bold (F1a) count markdown headers and bold spans. List markers (F1b) count bullet and number markers at the start of a line. A closing summary (F2) is a final paragraph that opens with a summary phrase, and a resource list (F4) is a final list of links or references. Each deletion rewrite removes only the markup or the span of its good and keeps every other character. When we delete all formatting at once, we also join the lines of a list into one paragraph. Appendix Q A Recipe for Comparing Reward Models before Training 1. Sample n ≈ 16 n\approx 16 responses for each of a few hundred prompts from the current policy, with the decoding settings of RL. 2. Score every response with the current and the candidate reward model, and standardize each model with the constants that RL will use. 3. For every behavior f f of interest, compute the within-prompt covariance Cov x ⁡ ( f , Δ ​ r ) \operatorname{Cov}_{x}(f,\Delta r) and average it over prompts. Divide by the within-prompt standard deviation of f f to compare behaviors. 4. Do the same for any available measure of quality, such as a stronger judge, to obtain the direction of the change in quality. 5. Read the signs as the forecast of direction. For sizes, fit one coefficient per behavior on a past edit of the same pipeline. 6. Track the KL divergence from the starting policy during RL. Once the divergence is large, the forecast of rent may no longer hold. 7. At a checkpoint, repeat steps 1–3 on samples of the current policy.
