Yuhua Jiang Tsinghua University yh-jiang24@mails.tsinghua.edu.cn Li Zhao * Microsoft Research lizo@microsoft.com Abstract Self-evolving agents improve through repeated cycles of modification and evaluation.
However, existing methods receive feedback on modifications only after costly evaluations, and aggregate scores may obscure distinctions between failures that require different repairs.
We introduce predictive self-evolution through an editable Improvement Dynamics Model (IDM).
Before evaluation, the IDM predicts task-level outcomes of a proposed modification and guides an optional revision.
After evaluation, past modifications and their observed outcomes are used to improve the IDM, allowing predictive knowledge to accumulate across generations.
The system can thus improve how it solves tasks, proposes modifications, and predicts their effects.
Building on HyperAgents, we evaluate this approach across multilingual coding, paper review, robotics reward design, and Olympiad-level mathematical grading.
Our approach outperforms HyperAgents across all four domains, with up to 46% higher average performance over generations.
Gains also persist under matched token budgets that include both agent evolution and task evaluation, and extend to held-out tasks.
Forecast accuracy improves as the IDM evolves, and the learned predictive knowledge transfers to improve evolution in a new domain. 1 1 1 Code is available at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/Predictive-self-evolution/ . 1 Introduction The capabilities of modern AI agents depend not only on their foundation models, but also on the programs that determine how they reason, use tools, maintain memory, and interact with their environments. Recent work on agent self-evolution seeks to improve these programs automatically by repeatedly modifying them, evaluating the resulting agents on target tasks, and using the evaluation results to guide later modifications ( Zhang et al., 2025a ; Zhang et al., 2026b ) . Yet, like Odysseus navigating without Athena, these systems rely on costly evaluations to discover the effects of their modifications. Predicting those effects could help refine proposals before evaluation. Past evaluations provide a basis for such predictions.
The Darwin Gödel Machine (DGM) ( Zhang et al., 2025a ) maintains an
archive of evaluated coding agents that can serve as parents for new branches.
Related methods share experience across branches or expose richer
historical evidence ( Weng et al., 2026 ; Lee et al., 2026 ) .
HyperAgents ( Zhang et al., 2026b ) extend DGM by combining a task agent and a meta agent in a single editable program.
The task agent solves the target tasks and is evaluated on them, while the meta agent modifies both the task agent and itself.
With access to the archive and prior evaluations, the meta agent can use experience from earlier generations to improve both task-solving behavior and the process that generates future modifications. Recent methods use this experience to guide proposal generation or anticipate outcomes.
DREvo ( Guo et al., 2026 ) uses past modifications and their outcomes
to guide new proposals, while AHE ( Lin et al., 2026 ) records
task-level expectations and checks them against subsequent evaluations.
AgentSquare ( Shang et al., 2025 ) ranks recombined agent
candidates with an in-context performance predictor.
These approaches do not, however, use predicted task-level outcomes
as feedback for revising a draft before evaluation.
We therefore seek to turn accumulated experience into predictive feedback
that helps refine proposals before costly evaluation. Using predictions to guide revision requires anticipating how a
modification will affect individual tasks, not just whether it will
improve the overall score.
Building on HyperAgents ( Zhang et al., 2026b ) , we introduce
predictive self-evolution with an Improvement Dynamics Model (IDM),
an editable program that invokes a frozen LLM to forecast task-level
outcomes from the current agent, a proposed code change, and historical
experience.
These forecasts preserve distinctions relevant to revision:
an incorrect attempt and a missing output, for example, may receive
the same failure score but call for different repairs.
The predicted aggregate score determines whether to request one
additional revision from the meta agent, while the task-level
forecasts guide that revision.
The resulting descendant’s task agent is then evaluated.
To improve forecasts as the agents themselves evolve,
the meta agent periodically edits
the IDM’s forecasting code and instructions using past modifications
and discrepancies between their predicted and observed outcomes
from across generations and branches.
Candidate updates are adopted only if they improve prediction
quality on subsequently collected outcomes.
Figure 1 illustrates this process. Figure 1: An Athena for self-evolution. Like Athena guiding Odysseus, the IDM anticipates task-level outcomes
to guide an optional revision before evaluation.
Only the resulting child’s task agent is evaluated.
Final forecasts and observed outcomes provide evidence for improving
the IDM across generations. Following HyperAgents ( Zhang et al., 2026b ) , we evaluate
predictive self-evolution across four domains: multilingual coding ( Gauthier, 2024 ) , paper review ( Zhao et al., 2026 ) ,
robotics reward design ( Genesis Authors, 2024 ) , and Olympiad-level
mathematical grading ( Luong et al., 2025 ) .
Our contributions are as follows. • Predictive self-evolution. We introduce an editable IDM that forecasts
task-level outcomes of proposed modifications
and guides selective revision before evaluation.
Its forecasting program evolves from experience
alongside the task and meta agents. • Effective use of evolution budgets. Allocating part of the budget to predictive feedback
improves evolution across four domains under matched
token budgets covering evolution and task evaluation.
Coding ablations support its value beyond an
additional revision alone. • Transferable predictive knowledge. Evolution produces predictive knowledge reusable
beyond the agents that generated it.
IDM forecasts improve with experience, and transferring
only the evolved IDM improves mathematical-grading
evolution over a freshly initialized IDM, with
identical initial task and meta agents. 2 Related Work Automatic agent design and self-evolution. Automatic agent design uses task feedback to search for effective programs and workflows built around foundation models.
Automated Design of Agentic Systems (ADAS) ( Hu et al., 2025 ) uses a meta agent to discover agent programs in code, while AFlow ( Zhang et al., 2025b ) searches over executable workflows using Monte Carlo tree search.
Reusing earlier discoveries is central to these searches.
The Darwin Gödel Machine ( Zhang et al., 2025a ) maintains an archive of evaluated coding agents that can serve as starting points for further self-modification, while Group-Evolving Agents ( Weng et al., 2026 ) organize evolution around groups that share experience across agents.
HyperAgents ( Zhang et al., 2026b ) extend evolution to the meta agent responsible for generating modifications, allowing the improvement procedure itself to change alongside the task agent.
We extend HyperAgents with an editable IDM that evolves alongside the task and meta agents. Learning from feedback and experience. Self-Refine ( Madaan et al., 2023 ) uses self-generated
feedback to revise the current output, while Reflexion ( Shinn et al., 2023 ) retains verbal reflections to guide
later trials.
Other approaches organize experience for repeated reuse:
Agentic Context Engineering (ACE) ( Zhang et al., 2025c ) maintains evolving contextual playbooks, and ReasoningBank ( Ouyang et al., 2025 ) distills reusable reasoning
strategies from successful and failed experiences.
Knowledge-Centric Self-Improvement (KSI) ( Wang et al., 2026 ) accumulates insights in a shared knowledge base that generic
agents use across tasks.
Beyond supplying context for task execution, feedback can
also guide changes to the agent’s prompts and programs.
GEPA ( Agrawal et al., 2026 ) reflects on execution trajectories
to propose and test prompt updates and combine complementary
improvements.
For program modification, Meta-Harness ( Lee et al., 2026 ) gives the optimizer access to
previous candidates’ source code, scores, and execution traces.
HarnessFix ( Chen et al., 2026 ) and Self-Harness ( Zhang et al., 2026a ) use execution traces to diagnose
failures and validate targeted harness repairs, whereas
DREvo ( Guo et al., 2026 ) reassesses historical evidence
against the current program before distilling it into
search directions. Predictive models for agent improvement. Predictive models have long been used to guide search and
reduce evaluation costs, including in surrogate-assisted
evolutionary optimization ( Jin, 2011 ) and
predictor-based neural architecture search ( Luo et al., 2018 ) .
At the program level, Neural Change Prediction ( Plein et al., 2026 ) learns from synthetic code
mutations and their observed effects to predict changes
in program outputs.
At the task-execution level, WorldEvolver ( Zhang et al., 2026c ) forecasts environment
transitions to guide actions and updates predictive memory
from discrepancies between forecasts and observations.
For agent optimization, predictions can support candidate
selection or make the expected effects of edits explicit.
AgentSquare ( Shang et al., 2025 ) uses an in-context
performance predictor to rank candidates formed by
recombining existing agent modules, reducing the need
for direct evaluation.
AHE ( Lin et al., 2026 ) records expected fixes and regressions
for each edit and checks these predictions against
subsequent task-level outcomes.
Our IDM instead uses forecasts of task-level evaluation
outcomes as feedback for revising a proposed agent-program
modification before evaluation.
The meta agent also edits the IDM’s forecasting code
and instructions using past modifications and prediction errors. 3 Predictive Self-Evolution 3.1 Archive-Based Self-Evolution Agents and task evaluation. Following HyperAgents ( Zhang et al., 2026b ) , an agent is any computable program.

evaluation and pairs the final forecast with the observed child
outcomes for subsequent IDM updates. 3.4 Evolving the IDM Task performance guides the evolution of task and meta agents,
but does not directly measure the IDM’s prediction accuracy.
We therefore update the IDM separately from the task and meta
agents and share it across archive branches, allowing records
from different branches to improve the same forecasting program.
As illustrated in the lower part of
Figure 2 , every K ≥ 2 K\geq 2 generations, the meta agent in the selected Hyperagent uses
the accumulated records to edit the current IDM’s forecasting
code and instructions, producing a candidate.
We validate the candidate on the next K K records, collected
while the current IDM remains deployed.
Once the block is complete, the candidate makes its forecasts
using the recorded pre-evaluation inputs, without access to
later archive updates or the corresponding child outcomes. To replace the current IDM, a candidate must reduce prediction
loss in the task forecasts used to guide revision.
We measure this loss using Brier loss ( Brier, 1950 ) for probabilities and squared
error for continuous values, with domain-specific definitions
given in Appendix A.4 .
For the score that determines whether to revise, we also require
that centered mean squared error (CMSE) ( Taylor, 2001 ) does not increase.
CMSE measures score prediction error after removing each IDM’s
mean bias on the validation block.
If both conditions hold, the candidate replaces the current IDM;
otherwise, the current IDM remains deployed.
Upon replacement, the candidate’s mean bias on this block
initializes the online calibration offset b b described in
Section 3.3 . 4 Experiments We evaluate whether predictive self-evolution improves agent performance under generation and token budgets.
We then examine forecast accuracy during evolution,
cross-domain transfer of predictive knowledge, and the
contributions of individual components through coding ablations. 4.1 Experimental Setup Initialization. We use the initial Hyperagent from HyperAgents ( Zhang et al., 2026b ) , adding instructions that distinguish
Hyperagent modifications from IDM updates and specify
the feedback available in each phase.
The initial IDM is instructed to predict task-level
outcomes from proposed modifications and available evidence.
Implementation details and instruction templates are
provided in Appendix A . Domains and evaluation protocol. Following HyperAgents ( Zhang et al., 2026b ) , we evaluate predictive self-evolution across four domains:
Polyglot coding ( Gauthier, 2024 ) ,
paper review ( Zhao et al., 2026 ) ,
robotics reward design in Genesis ( Genesis Authors, 2024 ) ,
and Olympiad-level mathematical grading using IMO-GradingBench ( Luong et al., 2025 ) .
Polyglot is evaluated independently, paper review and robotics are optimized jointly, and mathematical grading is the target setting for IDM transfer.
Each method is evaluated over five complete runs per setting,
following HyperAgents ( Zhang et al., 2026b ) .
Each run lasts 40 generations.
Within each setting, methods share the same initial Hyperagent, underlying task-agent and meta-agent LLMs, task splits, evaluation protocol, and parent-selection rule.
Domain-specific evaluation protocols are detailed in Appendix A , and IDM prediction formats and losses in Appendix A.4 . Baselines and variants. We refer to our method as Selective IDM in the experiments.
HyperAgents serves as the common baseline across the domains.
It evaluates the modified agent without requesting an additional revision from the meta agent.
On Polyglot, Reflective HyperAgents revises every draft once without IDM feedback.
We also evaluate two variants of our method on this benchmark.
Frozen Selective IDM retains selective revision and online bias calibration but fixes the IDM to its initial implementation.
IDM Reflection uses feedback from the evolving IDM to revise every draft once.
For mathematical grading, Selective IDM starts from the initial IDM, while Transferred IDM starts from an IDM evolved jointly on paper review and robotics.
Both conditions use the same evolution procedure and initial task and meta agents. Metrics and budgets. Task performance is measured by the evaluation score defined for each domain.
The joint score is the equally weighted mean of paper-review
validation accuracy and robotics training fitness.
To assess evolution efficiency, we use the normalized area under the budget curve (AUBC), which captures the benefit of discovering strong agents earlier.

for budgets of 20–40 generations. 5 Conclusion We introduced predictive self-evolution with an editable
IDM that learns from past modifications and their outcomes
to guide revision before evaluation.
By making the forecasting program itself an object of evolution,
the system can accumulate and reuse predictive knowledge
as the agents continue to change.
Experiments across four domains show more effective
evolution under matched budgets and support the transfer
of predictive knowledge to a new domain.
Forecast accuracy also improves with experience.
Extending the IDM toward a world model of agent evolution
could help identify modification sequences whose early steps
offer little immediate gain but enable greater improvements
in later generations. AI use statement In this work, we used generative AI tools for method implementation
