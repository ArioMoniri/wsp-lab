Abstract
Trained network weights are increasingly treated as data, but there is rarely ground
truth about what a population of models should encode, which hampers systematic
validation of weight-space methods. This makes parametric dynamical systems a
natural test-bed: the dynamics change qualitatively at submanifolds of parameter
space that are, in well-studied families, known analytically or numerically. Finetuning a forecasting network at each point of a parameter grid yields a model zoo
indexed by a physical parameter, against which weight-space diagnostics can be
checked.
We build such model zoos for four dynamical systems and analyse them with
JEPAWG, a recently proposed joint-embedding hypernetwork built around a learned
parameter–weight representation. It was first introduced for neural samplers for
lattice field theories, where its latent geometry located the phase transition and
estimated the 2D Ising critical exponent. Applied to dynamical systems, JEPAWG
is competitive on forecasting with a hypernetwork trained directly for that task,
despite being supervised on the parameter–weight correspondence rather than on
forecasts. Beyond forecasting, the norm of the parameter-derivative of the decoded
weights locates Hopf points and curves, tracks a period-doubling cascade closely
enough to give a qualitative estimate of the Feigenbaum constant, and detects a
global bifurcation, which has no eigenvalue signature and is therefore invisible to
linearisation. As such, JEPAWG provides interpretable insight into the dynamics
that is not available from other hypernetworks. Together, these results indicate that
JEPAWG is a workable instrument for reading physical structure off a population
of trained networks.

Introduction

With the growing number and scope of trained neural networks, the weights have increasingly become
an object of study themselves. Collections of networks trained under controlled variation, model
zoos [1], are used as datasets in their own right: to predict properties of a network from its weights,
to average models into stronger ones (model soups [2]), or to learn representations of weight space
itself. A related line of work builds hypernetworks [3] generating the weights of a target network
from a conditioning input, treating weights not as data but as the output to be produced.
A model zoo becomes a parametrised model zoo when each of its members is associated with a
value of a continuous task parameter p. Because the task the members are trained for differs only

through p, such a zoo carries information about the system the task operates in, and analysing the zoo,
rather than any single network in it, becomes a way to study that system. JEPAWG (JEPA Weight
Generation) [4] realises this vision through an interpretable hypernetwork: it learns a joint latent
space of model weights and task parameters with a joint-embedding predictive architecture (JEPA)
objective [5]. Afterwards, it trains a decoder from this latent back to weight space, which enables the
generation of weights at unseen parameters. The parameter derivative of the decoded weights exposes
qualitative changes in the underlying dynamics. JEPAWG was first applied in lattice field theory,
where its latent space was shown to locate the Ising phase transition and estimate its critical exponent.
Here we consider parametric dynamical systems, which provide a natural test-bed: data generation
is cheap, and the dynamics change qualitatively on bifurcations, submanifolds of parameter space
whose location is, for the families we study, known analytically or numerically.
In this work we build parametrised model zoos for four dynamical systems, train the JEPAWG
architecture, and study the result from two perspectives: as a hypernetwork, by evaluating the
generated forecasters at unseen parameters, and as a probe of the dynamics, by testing whether
the derivative of the decoded weights with respect to the control parameters localises the known
bifurcations. We find that JEPAWG is competitive with an end-to-end hypernetwork on held-out
interpolation parameters, despite never being trained on the forecasting loss. As a probe for the
underlying dynamical systems, the weight-space response localises Hopf points and curves, resolves a
period-doubling cascade, and registers a global bifurcation. Our main contributions are the following.
A weight-space benchmark with known ground truth Parametric dynamical systems supply
an independent statement of what a population should encode. Crossing a bifurcation qualitatively
changes the dynamics, so networks trained on either side should differ in weight space. We construct
zoos for four families with established bifurcation structure, against which weight-space diagnostics
can be validated: the Brusselator Hopf curve bc = 1 + a2 , the Lorenz Hopf locus ρH (σ) at β = 8/3,
the period-doubling cascades of the Rössler oscillator and the Hénon map, whose accumulation
should recover the Feigenbaum constant δ ≈ 4.6692.
Detection of local bifurcations The parameter derivative of the decoded weights peaks where the
dynamics are most sensitive to the parameter, which happens near local bifurcations. For the Lorenz
system (σ = 10, β = 8/3) the peak lies at ρ = 24.51 ± 0.23, inside the bistable window preceding
the subcritical Hopf point ρH ≈ 24.74, and tracks ρH (σ) when both parameters are swept. The
response also locates the supercritical Hopf curve of the Brusselator, and in the Hénon map it resolves
the first five period doublings accurately enough to provide a qualitative estimate of the Feigenbaum
constant δ.
Detection of a global bifurcation Global bifurcations, unlike local bifurcations, are not visible in
the linearisation and traditionally require computing invariant manifolds or scanning attractor-level
observables. The same derivative again detects the Lorenz Bykov T-point, at which every fixed point
retains its stability. This shows that information inaccessible to a Jacobian computation can be read
off the geometry of the JEPAWG-generated weights.
The decoded map as a regulariser of the weight grid The fine-tuned weights carry per-run
training noise that dominates their parameter derivative. The decoder, fitted on a fixed embedding
of the parameter, does not reproduce this noise (Fig. 3a), and the decoded weights retain accurate
forecasts for longer than the fine-tuned weights on all four systems (Table 5). The JEPA-style latent
is organised by dynamical regime, has an intrinsic dimension that is close to dim p (Fig. 4b, Table 4),
and is insensitive to parameters that do not enter the dynamics (Appendix B, Fig. 8).

Related Work

Trained weights as data. Model zoos [1] assemble populations of networks trained under controlled
variation, and permutation-aware encoders and neural functionals [6, 7] learn structure over weight
space directly. More recently Falk et al. [8] argues that useful weight-space representations can be
learned from unstructured public model repositories, without curated zoos. That line targets prediction
of model properties across heterogeneous populations. We study a single population indexed by
a physical parameter, where known bifurcation structure provides ground truth for weight-space
diagnostics.
2

Hypernetworks for dynamical systems. Hypernetworks, networks that generate the weights of
another network [3], have recently been applied to parametric dynamical systems. PHLieNet [9]
learns a continuous parameter-to-weights map by back-propagating through the forecasting loss,
generalising recurrent forecasters that take p as an additional input. It is indexed by the control
parameter, but its weights are constrained only by forecasting accuracy, and in our experiments its
parameter derivative shows no discernible bifurcation signal (Section 5.2.1). CoDA [10] adapts a
shared forecaster through context-dependent low-rank weight corrections, and TiDHy [11] generates,
from a latent state, the weights that mix several linear systems evolving on different timescales. Both
condition on a variable inferred from trajectories rather than on the control parameter, so their weights
are not indexed by a directly interpretable physical quantity. In each case the weights are a means to
accurate prediction. In this work forecasting remains a requirement, but the map from parameter to
weights carries the additional role of a diagnostic.
Transitions read off learned models. Machine learning has been used to locate phase transitions
without a known order parameter, from classifiers trained on raw configurations [12] to the accuracy
of classifiers trained on deliberately mislabelled data [13]. Closer to the present work are approaches
that read the transition off the parameters of trained networks rather than their outputs. Tanaka
and Tomiya [14] observed that the weights of a network trained to predict the temperature of Ising
configurations organise into two domains whose boundary approximates the critical temperature.
Hernandes et al. [15] fine-tune neural quantum states adiabatically across a phase diagram, so that the
resulting weights are strongly correlated, and detect quantum phase transitions as structure in weight
space. JEPAWG [4] instead learns a map from couplings to the weights of normalising flows for a
scalar lattice field theory through a JEPA-style [5] latent space, whose geometry locates the phase
transition and encodes a finite-size shift consistent with the 2D Ising critical exponent. In the present
work we transfer this approach to dynamical systems, where the known bifurcation structure provides
ground truth.
Within dynamical systems, data-driven approaches to bifurcations have stayed close to local,
equilibrium-based theory. Bury et al. [16, 17] train classifiers on noisy normal-form simulations
to detect the critical slowing down that precedes a bifurcation in a time series and predict its type,
including period doubling in discrete-time maps. Mogharabin and Ghadami [18] fit a normal form
on a learned low-dimensional invariant manifold, and Pal and Melnik [19] locate saddle-nodes from
a learned equilibrium-to-parameter map trained on the governing equations. All of these target
bifurcations of a single equilibrium, and Bury et al. [17] explicitly leave global bifurcations outside
their scope. We instead differentiate a map from the parameter to the weights of a model of the
full dynamics. This requires no order parameter, linearisation or governing equations, and remains
defined for transitions that are not a property of any single equilibrium or periodic orbit, such as
global bifurcations [20, 21].

Background

Parametric dynamical systems and bifurcations Consider a parametric dynamical system whose
evolution is governed by

where x ∈ RDx is the state, t is time, and p ∈ RDp is a vector of control parameters. Trajectories
can be obtained with any integration scheme (e.g. Euler or Runge–Kutta). For discrete-time maps
the evolution is given by xn+1 = F (xn ; p), producing a discrete sequence of states instead of a
continuous path.
The long-time behaviour of such a system is characterised by its attractors, the sets toward which
trajectories converge. Depending on the parameter p, different types of attractors can arise: fixed
points, limit cycles (periodic orbits), or strange attractors, on which the dynamics are chaotic. As the
control parameters vary, an attractor can lose stability and the dynamics reorganise qualitatively; such
a change is called a bifurcation. The submanifolds of parameter space on which bifurcations occur
partition it into regions of qualitatively distinct dynamics [22, 23]. Two types recur in this work: the
Hopf bifurcation, in which a fixed point loses stability, and the period-doubling bifurcation, in which
a periodic orbit of period τ is replaced by one of period 2τ . Many systems reach chaos through an
infinite cascade of such doublings.

3

Local bifurcations, including the two types mentioned above, happen at a single fixed point or orbit
and are visible in the linearisation there: a Hopf bifurcation is an eigenvalue pair of the Jacobian
crossing the imaginary axis, a period doubling a Floquet multiplier crossing −1. Such conditions
can be solved in closed form or tracked numerically, which is how the analytic reference values we
validate against in this work are obtained [23].
Global bifurcations admit no such local criterion: there is no single fixed point or orbit whose
linearisation registers the change. They instead involve the invariant manifolds of a saddle. A
saddle carries an unstable manifold, the trajectories that emerge from it, and a stable manifold, the
trajectories that converge to it. Both extend far into state space. A one-dimensional unstable manifold
generically misses the stable manifold of a saddle, since their coincidence is one scalar condition
on the parameters; at isolated parameter values, however, it lands exactly on one, either of the same
saddle (a homoclinic connection) or of another (a heteroclinic connection). When this happens the
global arrangement of trajectories reorganises, yet every fixed point keeps its stability. As a result
there are no eigenvalue signals for this change. Finding global bifurcations therefore traditionally
requires computing the invariant manifolds themselves, or scanning attractor-level observables across
parameter space.
In this work we locate both classes by a route that uses neither the linearisation nor an explicit analysis
of trajectories. At each parameter value pi we train a small network (∼ 104 weights) to forecast the
dynamics, so that the model zoo supplies a family of trained weight vectors {θ(pi )}. Each network is
fine-tuned only on trajectories generated at its own pi , so its weights encode how the system behaves
there; when the dynamics reorganise, the weights must change rapidly. A bifurcation is then visible
as a large sensitivity of the weights to the parameter, a quantity we make precise in Section 4.3.
The first step is to build a forecasting model, by which we mean a function that predicts the future
evolution of the state from a short observed history together with the parameter values p. We call
such a function a parametric forecaster, and specify the architecture we use below.

3.1

Forecasting Hypernetworks

A forecaster can be made aware of the parameter vector p in several ways. The simplest is not to
condition on p at all and to let the model infer the dynamics from the state history alone. Alternatively,
extending the state vector as x′ = [x, p] gives the model direct knowledge of the parameter setting.
As a third option, we consider a hypernetwork: a separate neural network that learns to map a given
parameter to a weight vector, p 7→ θ. The resulting weights θ then parametrise the forecasting
network fθ , which predicts the state evolution.

Methods

This section describes the pipeline in three steps (Figure 1). Section 4.1 builds the model zoos:
trajectories are generated across a parameter range, a single p-conditional forecaster is trained on
all of them, and that network is fine-tuned at each selected point to give the weights {(pi , θi )}M
i=1 .
Section 4.2 fits JEPAWG to that collection of weights. Section 4.3 defines the two ways we evaluate
the resulting map p 7→ θ̂(p): as a forecaster at unseen parameters, and as a probe of the dynamics
through its parameter derivative.
4.1

Data Generation

In principle, the zoo could be built by training a separate forecaster from scratch at every parameter
value. We instead train one p-conditional TCNN-CD [29] on the pooled trajectories and use it as the
initialisation for a further fine-tune at each pi . Besides amortising the training cost, this addresses a
symmetry of the problem. The loss is invariant under a large symmetry group, including permutations
of channels within a layer and rescalings across ReLU layers, so forecasters trained independently at
neighbouring parameter values would generically land in different symmetry copies of an equivalent
solution, and the difference between their weight vectors would be dominated by that choice rather
5

than by the change in the dynamics. A common initialisation acts as an approximate gauge fixing.
The hope is that fine-tuning every θi from the common conditional network, with a short schedule at
a fixed learning rate, keeps the weights within a common symmetry branch, so that variation along
the grid reflects the parameter dependence of the dynamics.
Trajectory generation For each system we sample M parameter values by scrambled Sobol
sequences [31] over the control range (per-system ranges are given in Table 1, and densities and
stratification in Table 6). At each parameter p we generate trajectories from several random initial
conditions. Continuous-time systems are integrated with fixed-step RK4 at solver step δt = 10−3 ;
the observation interval, recorded length, and number of initial conditions are per-experiment and are
listed in Table 6. Discrete maps are iterated directly (∆t = 1). To mitigate dependence on the initial
conditions we discard an initial transient for some systems, integrated in addition to the recorded
lengths above; durations per system are listed in Table 6. States are standardised per state dimension,
with mean and variance computed over the pooled training trajectories; training and rollouts operate
in this scaled space, and all metrics are computed after inverting the standardisation, in the original
state space.
Conditional forecaster We first train a single p-conditional TCNN-CD on the trajectories from
all parameter values. The conditioning isPimplemented by appending p to every state, x′ = [x, p],
and the network is trained by minimising i Lpi (θ) with the loss (3). This conditional base network
learns the dynamics shared across the parameter range and serves as a common initialisation for the
per-parameter fine-tuned networks below. Training windows are perturbed by additive Gaussian input
noise with standard deviation 5% of the standardised feature scale, redrawn each batch and applied to
the input window only. Target states are computed from the clean trajectories, and evaluation uses
clean inputs. This trains the network to contract the small state perturbations it feeds itself during
iterated predictions, following [9].
Fine-tuning Initialised from the conditional base, the network is fine-tuned on the windows of
each parameter value pi separately for 30 epochs with Adam at learning rate 10−3 , under the same
objective and input noise as pre-training, giving target weights θi ∈ RDθ . Each member keeps
the standardised parameter as an extra input channel, inherited from the conditional base; since
it is fine-tuned at a fixed pi , this channel is constant during fine-tuning, and all p-dependence the
member learns is stored in θi . The resulting weight grid {(pi , θi )}M
i=1 is flattened and standardised
per dimension.
4.2

JEPAWG Training

JEPAWG is then trained on the parameter–weight pairs {(pi , θi )}M
i=1 assembled above, in the two
stages described in Section 3.1 (Figure 1, panel 2). All components Ep , P, Eθ , D are taken to
be MLPs with ReLU activations. The latent dimension is dz = 16, following [4]: the latent is
deliberately over-sized relative to the dimension of the parameter manifold, so that any concentration
onto fewer dimensions is a learned property rather than an architectural constraint. Note that Ep is
not a compression: p is one- or two-dimensional here, so the parameter branch expands rather than
reduces, and the name encoder is inherited from the joint-embedding setting rather than descriptive.
The weight encoder Eθ and decoder D both have two hidden layers of size 512 each. The parameter
encoder Ep maps a feature vector consisting of pi together with 40 sinusoidal encodings per parameter
axis (sine and cosine at 20 log-spaced frequencies; 128 encodings for the Hénon u-cascade) to the
16-dimensional latent space through a single hidden layer of size 64. The predictor P also contains
a single hidden layer of width 64. Loss hyperparameters are set to be winv = wvar = 25, wcov = 0,
with γ = 1. Both stages use Adam at learning rate 3 × 10−4 , batch size 256, and 5000 steps. We
train 5 independent seeds per configuration.

Analysis

We evaluate JEPAWG on four dynamical systems in two respects. First, we decode weights at unseen
parameters and compare the resulting forecasts against the true trajectories and against the baselines.
Accurate forecasts show that the decoded weights retain the information specifying the dynamics,
which we then probe by measuring how strongly the decoded weights respond to a small perturbation
of the parameters and comparing where this response peaks with the known bifurcations.

signature beyond the change in the invariant measure.

known, this provides a direct test of the resolution of G(p). We use the map in three configurations,
with different protocols (Table 6): a one-parameter a-sweep for forecasting, a two-parameter (a, b)
grid, and a separate zoo built along the cascade, described in Section 5.2.3.

field at a location that moves with the parameter. For Lorenz with ρ ∈ [5, 9], the forecasters generated

against 3.6 ± 0.3), whose vector field displaces or removes it. For the Brusselator with b ∈ [1.1, 1.4],
trajectories after the initial transient lie close to the fixed point, so the variance normalising the
NRMSE is small and even small offsets exceed the threshold. PHLieNet scores higher here, but
with a near-zero normaliser and one seed dominating the mean, the difference is not meaningful.

TtT is limited by the Lyapunov time rather than by how well the attractor itself is reproduced. For

Accurate forecasts from the decoded networks show that the map p 7→ θ̂(p) retains the information
specifying the dynamics, but this does not guarantee that this information is arranged in a way that
can be retrieved by differentiation. PHLieNet is such an example: it matches JEPAWG on forecasting
(Table 1) yet its generated weights carry no visible response at the Lorenz Hopf point (Section 5.2.1).
JEPAWG instead fits the parameter–weight correspondence directly, which is what the weight-space
analysis relies on.

Brusselator

The Brusselator displays a second two-parameter Hopf curve: whereas the Lorenz Hopf is subcritical,
with trajectories jumping onto the coexisting strange attractor, crossing b = 1 + a2 is supercritical,

varied, G(p) again traces the analytic curve (Fig. 4a). The same transition is visible in the learned
latent space: the top two principal components of the latent variable zw form a smoothly varying sheet
in which the two regimes occupy the two sides of an internal boundary, and the sheet is stretched
near the Hopf curve (Fig. 4b).

6

Conclusion

For four dynamical systems, we built model zoos of fine-tuned forecasting networks indexed by the
control parameters, and evaluated the parameter-to-weights map JEPAWG learns from them both as a
hypernetwork and as a probe of the dynamics. As a hypernetwork, JEPAWG is competitive with an
end-to-end baseline on held-out interpolation parameters across all four systems. Outside the training
range, which method performs better depends on the dynamics there. Where the limit cycles continue
to change, JEPAWG follows the trend of the weights and gives the better forecasts, most clearly
for Rössler with c ∈ [1.8, 2.8]. Where the dynamics settle onto a fixed point, PHLieNet, which
returns the forecaster of the nearest training parameter, preserves it more reliably. In chaotic regimes,
both methods lose the forecast within a few Lyapunov times. As a probe, the parameter derivative
of the decoded weights, G(p), recovers the subcritical Hopf point and curve of the Lorenz system
and the supercritical Hopf curve of the Brusselator, and registers the global bifurcation structure
around the Bykov T-point, which, unlike the Hopf curves, is invisible to any linearisation. Finally,
we used the weight-space response G(p) to localise the first five period doublings of the Hénon map
to within |∆u| ≤ 0.04, yielding a qualitative measurement of the Feigenbaum constant. JEPAWG
thus turns a parametrised model zoo into a single object serving two roles: a hypernetwork producing
functional forecasters at unseen parameters, and a probe of the dynamics that generated the zoo.
Having validated the weight-space response against known bifurcation structure, we see a promising
prospect in applying the method to systems whose dynamics are not yet fully understood, where the
weights would point to structures not yet identified, indicating where to concentrate simulation or
further training rather than sweeping a parameter range at uniform cost.
Limitations First, the probe can only register what is present in the training data. Transitions that
leave typical trajectories unchanged, such as bifurcations of non-attracting sets, are invisible to it.
Second, outside the range of the training parameters nothing constrains the decoded weights, and
how quickly the error destroys the forecast depends on the dynamics there. In chaotic regimes it
is amplified, and every method we tested loses the forecast within a few Lyapunov times. Where
the dynamics settle onto a fixed point, JEPAWG continues the trend of the weights and displaces or
removes the fixed point (Section 5.1). Third, G(p) responds to any rapid change in the distribution of
the training trajectories, so a peak indicates a reorganisation of the observed dynamics, not necessarily
a bifurcation. Its resolution is bounded by the average parameter spacing (Table 6) and by the
smoothness of the decoded map, and since G is a derivative, the location of its peaks depends on the
coordinates in which the parameter is expressed. Finally, the dominant cost is the zoo itself, which
requires M fine-tuned networks per system.

A

Architectural Details

Forecasting network The forecasting network throughout is the TCNN-CD [29] in the configuration of [9]: a causal temporal CNN with dilations 20 , . . . , 2L−1 (L chosen as the minimum depth
whose receptive field covers the input window, L=4 at input sequence length T = 32), 22 channels
per layer, kernel size 5, and a linear readout. PHLieNet uses the same TCNN-CD as its generated
target network, with a LIE of 32 anchors, embedding dimension 16, RBF bandwidth ℓ=0.2, and a
hypernetwork MLP of two hidden layers of width 64; it is trained with Ranger (RAdam + Lookahead)
[35], initial learning rate 10−2 , and the training protocol of [9].
Baselines We compare JEPAWG’s embedding against PCA, a deterministic autoencoder (AE), and
a variational autoencoder (VAE) on the weight grids of all four systems (layer sizes below are for
Lorenz, M =800). All methods use latent dimension dz =16 and weights normalised per-feature with
training-split statistics. PCA projects onto the top 16 training principal components; AE/VAE mirror
JEPAWG’s encoder/decoder capacity (7857→512→512→16 and back; VAE with βKL =10−3 ). Since
PCA/AE/VAE provide no parameter-to-latent map, each is paired with an identical post-hoc regressor
p → z (MLP 2×64, 5 000 Adam steps); JEPAWG uses its parameter path D(P (Ep (p))).

Intrinsic Dimension and Ablation

Intrinsic dimension We estimate the intrinsic dimension of each training-set latent with the
TwoNN maximum-likelihood estimator of Facco et al. [36] and the Levina–Bickel MLE (MacKay–
Ghahramani correction) [37] at neighbourhood sizes k ∈ {5, 10, 20}, probing increasingly coarse
scales.
On the two one-parameter grids, JEPAWG’s estimate decreases monotonically with scale (2.9 → 1.4
on Lorenz and 2.8 → 1.7 on Rössler (Table 4)); on the two-parameter Brusselator and Hénon grids
(d=2) the same descent levels off at 2.3 and 2.2 respectively. The other baselines generally show
much higher intrinsic dimension, further removed from the control dimension. The raw weight grids
themselves measure 12–20 (Lorenz), up to 17 (Rössler), and 4.5–6 (Brusselator, Hénon). JEPAWG’s
covariance-free VICReg objective acts as a regulariser, smoothing the weight variation that the control
encoder cannot predict, collapsing to a latent space that is more closely aligned with the underlying
manifold.

Decoded weights versus raw weights As an ablation, we evaluate the raw weights on the forecasting task and compare with the performance of the decoded weights. We observe that the decoded
weights outperform the raw weights in TtT on all four systems (Table 5), even though the decoder
is trained only to reconstruct them. A plausible explanation is that each θi carries noise specific to
the trajectories of its fine-tune; the smooth decoder cannot fit this per-point noise and would then
converge toward a denoised weight sheet, so that its reconstruction residual consists largely of the
component that harms forecasting.

As a second ablation, we freeze the encoders Ep , Eθ and the predictor P (Sec. 3.1) at initialisation
and train only the decoder D with the reconstruction loss (Eq. (5)). The frozen Eθ is then a random
projection of the weights whose latent shows no organisation by parameter (Fig. 7), in contrast to the
trained latent of Fig. 4b, where the same zoo lies on a surface parametrised by (a, b) that separates
the stable fixed point from the limit-cycle regime. The parameter encoder Ep effectively becomes
a random-feature embedding. On the Brusselator zoo this control reaches a reconstruction error
comparable to JEPAWG (random-feature MSE 0.0446 vs. JEPAWG 0.0441), and its response G on
the (a, b) grid correlates strongly with JEPAWG’s (Pearson r = 0.96), indicating that a similar map
p 7→ θ̂ is learned. However, a random-feature encoder fixes the geometry of the parameter latent
before any data are seen: every coordinate of p is embedded on the same footing, irrespective of its
effect on the dynamics. JEPAWG’s latent is instead shaped by the zoo through the invariance term,
which ties P (Ep (p)) to the weight-side latent Eθ (θ). A parameter that moves the weights spans a
latent direction, while one that does not is dropped. With a single parameter, the two embeddings
behave alike, since there is only one direction along which to allocate capacity. They separate as
soon as the coordinates of p are not on equal footing, which is the common case in more complicated
systems with several parameters of unequal influence, or with parameters that are recorded but do not
enter the dynamics.
We test the limiting case directly by appending k random parameters ξ ∼ U(0, 1)k to p = (ρ, ξ) on
the Lorenz zoo; they have no effect on the dynamics, and the zoo weights are unchanged. JEPAWG
is insensitive to ξ: the Hopf peak keeps its location (Fig. 8), and at most 0.1% of the squared gradient
of θ̂ lies along ξ. The random-feature control has no way to tell ξ apart from ρ and spends capacity
on it, so its peak near the Hopf point degrades as k grows, until G no longer reliably locates the
bifurcation. The forecasts reflect the same difference. At k = 10, the interpolation horizon of the
JEPAWG-decoded networks is 20.1 ± 0.2 time units, against 20.2 ± 0.2 at k = 0, while that of the
random-feature control drops from 20.2 ± 0.2 to 18.5 ± 0.5.

Compute Resources

All training and evaluation ran on single NVIDIA L4 GPUs on a university SLURM cluster. The
dominant cost is the construction of each weight grid: conditional pre-training followed by M
per-parameter fine-tunes takes 2–6 GPU-hours per grid, depending on trajectory length and grid size.
Training one JEPAWG seed on a finished grid takes ∼40 seconds, so the five-seed embedding stage
is negligible relative to grid construction; evaluation adds 5–20 GPU-minutes per system.
