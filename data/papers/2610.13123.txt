Abstract
A solid understanding of the predictive success of deep neural networks (DNNs)
remains elusive. For example, while many data-dependent metrics exist that seek
to predict model quality of DNNs, these metrics may be expensive to compute
for large datasets and/or may be impossible to compute when training datasets are
not released. A practical and effective predictor of model quality involves analyzing DNN weight matrices, as prior work has established that heavy-tailed spectral
distributions in trained weight matrices can be strongly predictive of model quality. However, such metrics lack a rigorous statistical justification. In this work,
we aim to understand why these metrics perform well, from a statistical perspective. To do so, we employ a probabilistic framework to derive the marginal likelihood for a DNN, evaluated using only the final layer weights. Across large-scale
convolutional DNNs and LLMs, we find that this marginal likelihood predicts
model quality. To understand the role heavy-tailed spectra play in predictive performance, we derive the limiting log-marginal likelihood for spectra that follow
the (heavy-tailed) High-Temperature Marchenko-Pastur (HTMP) distribution. We
show that this limiting quantity is convex in a heaviness parameter, and we derive
an optimal heaviness that empirically predicts over-fitting for large-scale DNNs.

bias-variance curve to the well-known double-descent curve (Belkin et al., 2019). The recent success of large language models (LLMs) further complicates the picture: foundation models are often
under-parameterized, and do not attain zero-loss (Hoffmann et al., 2022). Instead, the performance

Tools for attempting to estimate model quality of DNNs are extensive. Classical statistical techniques using complexity measures such as the Rademacher complexity, Vapnik–Chervonenkis (VC)
dimension, and uniform stability have been largely unsuccessful (Zhang et al., 2017). The celebrated PAC-Bayes bounds (McAllester, 2003) provide improved generalization bounds, yet do not
provide a sufficient explanation of generalization (Lotfi et al., 2022a; Livni & Moran, 2020). A related quantity is the marginal likelihood of a DNN, which is connected to both PAC-Bayes bounds
(Germain et al., 2016) and leave-k-out cross-validation (Fong & Holmes, 2020). Immer et al. (2021)
demonstrated correlation with model quality for large DNNs, although certain caveats have been reported (Lotfi et al., 2022b). This quantity is important in SLT (Konishi & Kitagawa, 2008), since
from it the Bayesian Information Criterion and Watanabe–Akaike Information Criterion can be derived. For singular models (e.g., DNNs), the Widely Applicable Bayesian Information Criterion
(Watanabe, 2013) was developed; it can predict quality of pre-training for downstream tasks (Munn
& Wei, 2025). These information criteria are derived in the regime where number of data-points
n → ∞, for fixed parameter dimension p; but this is no longer a valid assumption for modern deep
learning (Couillet & Liao, 2022). To account for this, the Interpolating Information Criterion (IIC)
(Hodgkinson et al., 2023b,c) was developed for models that attain zero-loss, although we should
note that the IIC depends on an implicit regularizer for DNNs, whose full characterization remains
a challenging open problem.
These techniques for predicting model quality are data-dependent, which presents multiple issues
for their practical use. Firstly, computation on massive training sets can become infeasible (Ameli
et al., 2025a,b). Secondly, foundation models are often trained on undisclosed or proprietary datasets
(Singh et al., 2025), limiting the ability for comparisons across frontier labs. In response, a novel
line of work has considered using DNN weight matrices in isolation (data-independent, i.e., without
training or test data) for predicting model quality (Martin & Mahoney, 2020; Yang et al., 2023).
Motivation arose from the Heavy-Tailed Mechanistic Universality (HT-MU) (Martin & Mahoney,
2019, 2020) and Heavy-Tailed Self-Regularization (HT-SR) theories (Martin & Mahoney, 2021).
These related approaches are phenomenological, or semi-empirical, theories that are motivated by
statistical mechanics (Martin & Mahoney, 2017, 2021), and they are based on the empirical observation that spectra of trained DNN weight matrices exhibit heavy-tails. Metrics derived from the
“heaviness” of the spectral tail are surprisingly strong predictors of model quality for both computer
vision (Martin et al., 2021) and language models (Yang et al., 2023). Such metrics can identify DNN
layers that are “under-trained,” allowing for layer-wise learning (Zhou et al., 2023) or pruning (Lu
et al., 2024). Motivated by this, Hodgkinson et al. (2025) recently developed the High-Temperature
Marchenko-Pastur (HTMP) spectral distribution, which (among other things) is appropriate for modeling (heavy-tailed spectra of) weight matrices after training. This probabilistic model exhibits
heavy-tailed spectra for weight matrices that do not necessarily possess heavy-tailed elements, a
phenomenon observed in practice (Martin & Mahoney, 2021), and it can reproduce neural scaling
laws for generalization error.

Related work & background

RMT in deep learning. RMT broadly studies the limiting spectrum of matrix-valued probabilistic
models. There is a long line of works that derive deterministic equivalents and generalization error
for linear or random DNNs using RMT (Liao et al., 2020; Mei & Montanari, 2022; Wang et al.,
2024b; Atanasov et al., 2026). For a two-layer DNN, the generalization error after one gradient step
was derived in (Ba et al., 2022). The performance of a fine-tuned DNN linearization was predicted
through a Gaussian-like local spectral law (Wei et al., 2022). The limiting free energy was derived in
(Hodgkinson et al., 2023a) for Gaussian Processes (GP) with inner-product or radial-basis kernels,
and in Wilson et al. (2026) for GPs with kernels derived from DNNs, using spectra derived from
(Fan & Wang, 2020). RMT for deep learning has recently been reviewed (Liao & Mahoney, 2026).
In RMT, one considers the double asymptotic limit, i.e., for matrix A ∈ Rm×n , take m, n → ∞
such that m/n → γ ∈ (0, ∞).
Implicit regularization. While explicit forms of regularization for DNNs are often employed, certain aspects of a DNN such as architecture choice (e.g., depth) or optimization scheme (small-batch
SGD) may implicitly (Mahoney & Orecchia, 2011; Mahoney, 2012) bias a DNN towards parameter
values that generalize better. For homogeneous DNNs on linearly separable data, steepest descent
with respect to any norm ∥·∥ will implicitly minimize the parameter norm past the point of interpolation (Tsilivis et al., 2026). Small-batch SGD is thought to bias DNNs towards flatter minima during
training, improving generalization (Keskar et al., 2017), although this idea has received criticism
(Dinh et al., 2017; Yao et al., 2020, 2021). The Geometric Complexity (GC) (Dherin et al., 2022),
defined by a discrete version of the Dirichlet Energy (Jost, 2017), is a measurement of geometric
smoothness that encompasses many previous notions of smoothness and seems to be correlated with
model quality. A related object (Munn & Wei, 2025) is the Real Log Canonical Threshold from
the Singular Learning Theory of Watanabe (2009), and its local approximation, the Local Learning
Coefficient (Lau et al., 2025) which also seems to correlate with model quality. We emphasize that
these implicit regularization metrics depend on training data.
Heavy-tailed self-regularization (HT-SR). HT-SR (Martin & Mahoney, 2021) posits that the empirical spectral distributions (ESD) of trained weight matrices follow a power-law (PL) (ρ(u) ∝
u−α , u > umin , α > 1), or exponentially-truncated PL (ET-PL) (ρ(u) ∝ u−α e−λu , u > umin , α >
1, λ > 0) in the tail, due to complex correlations learnt during training, for some chosen minimum
eigenvalue umin . Using the fitted α value, in Martin et al. (2021), it was shown that for trained

3.2

Evaluation of free energy using final-layer weights

For trained features Φ, the Bayes Free Energy can be used to measure model quality; while this is
not novel, the following extension is: under certain assumptions, for training temperature τ → 0, the
Bayes Free Energy for well-trained features can be evaluated using only the final-layer weights W,
up to some additive terms that cancel when comparing Bayes Free Energy between models trained
on the same dataset. Taking τ → 0 concentrates the marginal likelihood on the sub-manifold of
interpolators; this concentration approach was used to derive the IIC (Hodgkinson et al., 2023b).
Assumption 1 (Interpolation). For trained features Φ, assume n ≥ m, dL > m and Rank(Φ) =
Rank(Y) = m. Further, assume that trained weights are given by W = Φ† Y, and that ΦW = Y.
This minimum-norm interpolating solution W = Φ† Y is key; W contains all necessary information
about the relationship between features and targets. These assumptions have strong motivations:
under our entropic regularization training framework, taking τ, σ 2 → 0 such that τ /σ 2 → 0 ensures
W → Φ† Y, and the same result applies in the deterministic optimization setting (Golub & Pereyra,

assumptions (Papyan et al., 2020), these results extend to over-parameterized neural networks. Using
this interpolating relationship, we can now evaluate (2) using only the final-layer weights.

Empirical evaluation

Here, we empirically demonstrate the correlation of (BFE-W) with model quality.
Computer vision (CV). We take a large number of high-performing models, across different architecture types (e.g., ResNet, DenseNet etc.) from the torchvision model zoo that have
been pretrained on ImageNet-1K, where test accuracy and model weights are provided. We set
λ∗ = m/Tr(WT W) for each model. In Figure 1 (left), we present (BFE-W) versus top-5 test

Heavy-tails and free energy

In the previous section, (BFE-W) was derived for the final-layer weights. In this section, we will
relax this condition, and consider (BFE-W) for all weights of a DNN. We will observe in the sequel
that (BFE-W) for deeper DNN weights can still correlate very well with model quality, and we
will give theoretical motivation (in Appendix F.2). Thus, equipped with a metric, (BFE-W), that
explicitly connects trained weights of a DNN to model quality, we will use the machinery of RMT
to investigate the question of why—from a statistical perspective—the spectral shape of heavytailed weights is so strongly predictive of model performance. We give details on the spectral shape
of trained weights; we then use this to analyze how (BFE-W) is optimized for certain spectral
parameters, under an RMT limit; and we conclude with empirical validation for large-scale DNNs.
4.1

Trained weight spectra

training, the spectrum of weight matrices is often well-modeled by the HTMP distribution (see
Hodgkinson et al. (2025, Proposition 5.3)), which admits heavy-tails.

Conclusion & discussion

HT-SR theory posits that the spectral distributions of DNN weight matrices can predict model quality. In this work, we validate this claim through a probabilistic framework; we derive a data-free
metric, the Bayes Free Energy evaluated using only the final-layer weights, and we empirically
show that this metric is strongly predictive of model quality, for both CV models and LLMs. To
understand why heavy-tailed weight matrices may generalize better, we derive the deterministic limiting quantity of this metric under the assumption that weight spectra follow the recently proposed
HTMPκ,γ,β,τ distribution. We then show that this free energy is convex in the scale-based heaviness parameter, h = κ/β, and that an optimal parameter h∗ exists. Once this optimal parameter
is attained, we show increasing κ monotonically decreases free energy (correlating with improved
model quality). We empirically find that CV model training correlates with optimizing h; in contrast, LLM models optimize h early, and instead increasing κ correlates with improved performance.
Thus, we provide results that mirror the findings of the HT-SR theory; that optimal model quality
can be observed in the shape of weight spectra.
Limitations & future work. There are several limitations of our work; some of these limitations
present clear paths for future work. Firstly, we note that (BFE-W) was only derived for finallayer weights, and for regression. Further, the dual formulation of (BFE-W) cannot immediately
be compared across models with different activation dimension dL . Providing a remedy to these
limitations is a promising avenue for future work. Further work could also involve extending (BFEW) to models that use Dropout before the final-layer, or Vision Transformer models, as these models
did not show correlation between (BFE-W) and model quality.
We also note that a limitation of our framework is that for CV models during training, the prior precision parameter λ must be tuned post-hoc to optimize correlation. Deriving λ a-priori would allow
one to measure HTMPκ,γ,β,τ parameters during training, and to early-stop when ht ≈ h∗ . Further
future work should also involve attempting to theoretically derive the HTMPκ,γ,β,τ parameters for
certain classes of DNNs, or to show how certain training mechanisms affect these spectral parameters. Our framework would then elucidate how these mechanisms optimize model quality, through
the free energy connection.

26

F.1

Connection of BFE-W to previous implicit regularization metrics

To understand why (BFE-W) correlates so strongly with model quality, we can understand its connection to previous measurements of implicit regularization. Firstly, m−1 Tr(WT W) is an HT-SR
weight metric. This term also provides a connection to the Geometric Complexity (Dherin et al.,
2022); and this term is implicitly regularized after interpolation for gradient flow training (Tsilivis
et al., 2026). Secondly, while the
 log-determinant is less common in the literature, a key term in
the IIC is n−1 log det Df Df T , where Df Df T is the empirical Neural Tangent Kernel (Jacot
et al., 2018). This measures the complexity of f through the volume of the distribution of functions
induced by parameter Jacobian of f . The second term in (BFE-W) instead measures the volume of
functions induced by the last-layer weights, i.e., a functional complexity, and is the information gain
for a Gaussian model with kernel WT W.

F.2

Motivation for application of BFE-W for deeper layer weights

While (BFE-W) was derived for final-layer weights, we can motivate the use for weights from
deeper layers. Specifically, for deeper weights Wl , (BFE-W) corresponds to the Bayes free energy
for an auxiliary Gaussian Process with precision matrix K = λWlT Wl , for artificial outputs of
whitened isotropic noise. Once interpolation has occurred, this model measures the inherent spectral
geometry of WlT Wl through the trace and log-determinant, and so it is directly measuring the
implicit bias inherent in the learned weights. Examining the spectrum of weights to measure implicit
model bias is a core aspect of HT-SR that motivated HTMP. Even though this auxiliary energy is
not the Bayes free energy for the original dataset and prior-likelihood pair, we see in Figure 3 that
for the third-layer convolutional weight of a ResNet50 trained on CIFAR-10, (BFE-W) correlates
surprisingly well with test accuracy during training, once training loss has plateaued. We see similar
results for ResNet18 and ResNet34 on CIFAR-10 in Figure 7. In Figure 11, we plot the correlation of
(BFE-W) with model quality across all layers, for ResNet50 on CIFAR-100 and all Pythia models.
Interestingly, we see that every deeper layer for ResNet50 correlates very well with model quality
(ρ > 0.9). For the Pythia models, the Dense weight matrices seem to correlate very well across all
layers and model sizes; however, for QueryKey weight matrices, the results are more mixed.

F.3

Choice of precision parameter λ

To select λ when comparing model quality with (BFE-W) across epochs of training t, we take two
general approaches:
1. As detailed in Remark 1, we set λt = dl /||Wl,t ||2F at each epoch t.
2. Let t∗ denote the epoch when test error is minimized. Then, we set λt = dl /||Wl,t∗ ||2F , i.e., λ
remains constant.
We find in our experiments that the second option works best for CV models. In fact, taking the
first option for λ results in a very poor correlation for the CV models. In comparison, both options
work for the LLM models. To see why, we note the following. By Lemma 7, µ1 = h/2γ. Thus,
h = 2γµ1 . As h∗ = 2γ/λ, then if we set λ = 1/µ1 , then h = h∗ . Hence, by selecting λt =
dl /||Wl,t ||2F , we are explicitly assuming that h is already at its optimal value h∗ . As seen in Figure 4
and Figure 8, h moving through h∗ directly correlates with under- and over-fitting in CV models;
taking λt = dl /||Wl,t ||2F destroys this behavior. In comparison, we see in Figure 5 and Figure 9
that for the LLM models, ht ≈ h∗t at each epoch. Hence, taking λt = dl /||Wl,t ||2F does not alter
the correlation. Similar behaviour has arisen in the literature: in Martin et al. (2021), the spectral
size ||Wl,t ||2F correlated strongly with model quality for well-trained networks within a model class,
alongside metrics such as the tail exponent α. In comparison, Yang et al. (2023) demonstrated that
for LLMs, ||Wl,t ||2F was anti-correlated with model quality, suggesting that spectral size plays a
differing role for measuring model quality of LLMs versus CV models.
Being able to correctly predict the optimal λ value, i.e., λt = dl /||Wl,t∗ ||2F , would enable our
framework to accurately measure the amount of overfitting during training, and it is an important
avenue for future work.
27

F.4

Relation of optimal HTMP parameters to heavy-tailed results in literature

We now detail similarities between the optimal scale-based heaviness derived in Theorem 3 and
Lemma 1 and those derived in the literature.
While previous work in the heavy-tailed literature consider mainly the tail behavior (Bartlett et al.,
2020; Agrawal et al., 2022), the fact that the first moment is proportional to h suggests that a distribution’s mean shares importance with tail behavior. This also suggests deeper connections between
heavy-tailed metrics and Geometric Complexity (Dherin et al., 2022), discussed previously. We see
that the optimal heaviness h∗ = 2γ/λ in Theorem 3 is a function of the aspect ratio γ; dependence
of heavy-tails on the aspect ratio was argued for in Hu et al. (2025). If we consider normalized heaviness h̄ = λκ/2γβ (i.e., λµ1 ), we find h̄∗ = 1. This is remarkably similar to the work of Bartlett
et al. (2020), where the minimum-norm solution for over-parameterized linear regression provides
good generalization performance if power-law exponent on the linear feature covariance is equal to
1. A similar finding for DNNs was shown in Agrawal et al. (2022). Defining h̄ = λκ/γβ, optimal
heaviness h̄∗ = 2 places us in the moderately-heavy tailed universal class of HT-SR, which is expected to perform well (Martin & Mahoney, 2021). The clear numerical connection between these
previous works and ours is surprising and motivating, despite our differing definitions and matrices.

H.1

Permuted label experiment

We now demonstrate that even on a task where no feature learning can occur, (BFE-W) closely
follows model quality; further, the HTMP analysis of Theorem 3 and Lemma 1 can still predict the
optimal model over training. For this task, we train ResNet18 on CIFAR-10 where all training labels
have been randomly permuted. Hence, training inputs xi are now independent of training outputs
yi . For this task, the Bayes optimal accuracy is 10%, i.e., random guessing for 10 classes. Instead of
accuracy, the appropriate metric to consider is cross-entropy on the test dataset. An optimal model
will produce a prediction of softmax(f (x∗i ; W, ϕ)) = [0.1]10 for each input x∗i in a test dataset.
In Figure 6 (top left) we see that HTMP still provides an excellent fit to the weight matrices in this
case. Here, the weight matrix used for analysis is the third-layer convolutional weight matrix. We
plot test error, empirical and asymptotic free energy against training epochs in Figure 6 (top right),
for λ∗ = 4.72, where we see a very strong correlation (ρ = 0.819). We mark with a dotted line the
epoch when training accuracy reaches 99%, which we loosely interpret as interpolation. While the
absolute scale of free energy does not differentiate between the untrained and trained models, once
we constrain to interpolating epochs, the free energy very closely predicts test error.
Importantly, we also see in Figure 6 (bottom left) that after interpolating on the permuted training
set, the optimal model corresponds to the one whose HTMP ratio h = κ/β is closest to h∗ (in
Figure 6(bottom right) we see that the assumptions of Lemma 1 are valid). Thus, even in this
artificial learning task, our framework allows us to find what spectral properties the model is trying
to optimize.

I.2

When last-layer free energy does not predict model quality

As detailed in Section 3.3, certain classes of models do not show strong correlation between (BFEW) and model quality for CV.
Dropout. Models that use dropout directly before the final-layer weights, such as VGG (Simonyan
& Zisserman, 2015), EfficientNet (Tan & Le, 2021), and MNASNet (Tan et al., 2019),
do not show good correlation. This could be because the addition of dropout violates
the features and weights decomposition of a neural network, or perhaps because dropout
prevents complex correlations in the final-layer weights from occurring.
